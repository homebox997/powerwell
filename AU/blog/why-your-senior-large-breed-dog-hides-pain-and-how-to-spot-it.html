Why Your Senior Large Breed Dog Hides Pain (And How to Spot It)

August 07, 2026 · 6 min read · PawWell Editorial

The myth: If hes not yelping, hes fine

Youve probably heard it at the dog park: Oh, my old boy doesnt seem sore at allhe never makes a sound. Its a common belief, but its a dangerous one, especially for owners of stoic large breeds. Our Labs, Goldies, Rotties and Ridgebacks have centuries of instinct telling them to mask pain. In the wild, showing hurt meant being targeted. That instinct hasnt gone away just because they now sleep on a mattress in the corner of the living room.

If youre waiting for a yelp before you act, you may be missing weeks or months of quiet suffering. Here in Australia, where our big dogs love a beach stroll or a daily bounce down the back steps, pain often shows up as tiny, easily dismissed changeschanges you can learn to read.

The core answer: Pain isnt a single shout

Pain in senior large-breed dogswhether from arthritis, spinal wear, dental trouble or organ discomfortis usually a slow, creeping thing. The signs are subtle: a new hesitation before jumping into the car, a dog that circles the bed three times instead of once, a Labrador who suddenly prefers the cool tiles to his favourite corner. The core thing to understand is that your dog wont necessarily cry out. But that doesnt mean theyre pain-free.

Think of pain as a dimmer switch, not an on-off light. It builds gradually, and your dog adapts on the outside while the discomfort grows on the inside. The good news? You can spot it early, and once you know what youre looking at, you can have a truly helpful conversation with your vet.

A real-life scenario (illustrative)

Illustrative scenario not a real patient.

Meg is a 9-year-old Golden Retriever from the Sunshine Coast. Her owner, Dave, noticed shed stopped bringing the ball back during their after-dinner ritual. Shed still run out, tail wagging, but then just stand and look at him. She was also slower getting up in the morningstook three goes instead of one fluid motion. Dave put it down to just being an old girl. It wasnt until his vet did a pain assessment (watching for tiny weight shifts, muscle tightness and eye tension) that he learned Meg had moderate hip and elbow osteoarthritis. She wasnt limping. She wasnt whimpering. But she was hurting every day.

Megs story is the rule, not the exception. Most large-breed seniors show pain through withdrawal and routine changes, not through drama.

How dogs mask painand what it means for you

Dogs are descended from pack animals that gained safety from looking strong. That evolutionary wiring means theyll suppress obvious signs until the pain is severe. Add to that a stoic breed temperament (think Rottweilers whod rather keep working, or Mastiffs who seem unfazed by anything), and youve got a perfect recipe for silent suffering.

So what does change? The clues are in the daily rhythm. A dog in pain may:

  • Change their lying-down posture: they might use the sphinx position more (elbows tucked back, chest on the ground) to relieve hip or back strain, or avoid lying on one side altogether.
  • Shift weight while standing: youll see them rest a back leg with the toe just touching the floor, or lean against the wall during dinner prep.
  • Become clumsy: slipping on timber floors or hesitating on the back steps isnt always normal ageingit can be a joint-pain avoidance strategy.
  • Show small temperament changes: a dog that once loved a pat might lean away, lip-lick or yawn when you reach for the collar. These are low-level stress signals, often the first outward whisper of chronic pain.

The key is to trust your gut while using a simple, repeatable checklist. You see your dog every dayyoure the best person to notice these quiet shifts.

Today you can do: At-home self-check checklist

Grab a cuppa, sit near your dog and run through these five observations tonight. No special tools neededjust your eyes and a calm moment.

  • Resting position scan: Watch for 2 minutes while he dozes. Does he shift position often (more than 3 times in 10 minutes)? Does he avoid lying on one hip? Unsettled rest can be a sign of persistent dull ache.
  • Getting up routine: Call him gently from lying down. Note whether he uses a puppy-type rise or a slow, forward-rocking effort. If he splays a back leg or leans heavily on the front end, thats worth noting.
  • Stairs and steps check: Is he still taking the back steps one at a time with both back legs together (a bunny hop), or pausing on the landing? Any new hesitation, even just a second, matters.
  • Touch test: Run your hand gently along the spine, over the hips and down each leg to the paw. Does he flinch, turn his head sharply, or tuck his tail? A dog in pain may brace the muscles before you even make contactlook for that early tensing.
  • Play and connection check: Does he greet you with the same relaxed ears-forward stance? A dog who avoids eye contact, brings toys but wont release them, or moves away when you try to pat their trouble spot may be guarding an area of pain.

Write down what you notice. A few scribbled notes on a scrap of paper can help your vet see the pattern more clearlyespecially if you mention when it happens (morning stiffness vs. post-walk soreness).

When to call your vet right away

Most pain in older large dogs builds slowly and can be managed with a proper plan. But some signs mean you shouldnt wait.

Mobility red lines seek urgent vet assessment:

  • Your dog cannot put any weight on a leg, or holds a leg up and wont use it at all.
  • There is obvious swelling, heat or a visible deformity after a fall or knockeven if it happened yesterday.
  • Sudden severe pain: vocalising (yelping, whining), flinching when touched, hiding away, or trembling without an obvious reason.
  • Your dog seems unable to stand, or collapses when trying to rise.

Do not give human pain medication. Ibuprofen, paracetamol and other human tablets are toxic to dogs and can cause severe kidney or liver damage. Ring your vet or the nearest after-hours emergency clinic instead.

A gentle word to carry with you

Worrying that your dog might be in pain and you missed itthats a heavy thought. But noticing is the first step, and by reading this, youve already started the shift. Chronic pain in senior large breeds is real, but its not a verdict. With a watchful eye, a few simple checks and a good vet partnership, you can give your old mate a lot more comfortable days. Theyve earned itand so have you.

Frequently Asked Questions

My dog still wags his taildoes that mean hes not in pain?
Not necessarily. Tail-wagging can be a social signal, a habit, or even a stress response. Many dogs with moderate arthritis still wag at their owner while limping. Look at the full pictureresting posture, movement ease, and appetitenot just the tail.

Can I give my dog over-the-counter pain relief from the chemist?
No. Human pain relievers like ibuprofen, naproxen or paracetamol are toxic to dogs and can be fatal even in small doses. Always use only a pain medication prescribed by your veterinarian for your specific dog.

When does arthritis pain become an emergency?
Arthritis itself is usually chronic, but an acute-on-chronic episodesudden inability to stand, a joint that looks twisted or grossly swollen, or a dog who cries and wont moveneeds urgent care. Use the mobility red lines above as a guide.
