Mitral Valve Disease in Dogs: A Guide for Australian Pet Owners

Mitral valve disease (MVD) is the most common acquired heart disease in dogs, affecting up to 75% of Cavalier King Charles Spaniels by age 10. This guide explains what MVD is, how it progresses, and the treatment options available in Australia.

💡 Key Takeaway

Mitral valve disease is manageable, especially when caught early. The EPIC Study proved that starting pimobendan before symptoms develop can extend life by approximately 15 months. Regular monitoring, appropriate medication, and lifestyle adjustments help dogs with MVD live well for years.

What is Mitral Valve Disease?

The mitral valve is one of four valves in your dog's heart, located between the left atrium and left ventricle. Its job is simple but critical: it ensures blood flows in one direction, preventing backflow when the heart contracts.

In mitral valve disease (also called myxomatous mitral valve disease or MMVD), the valve leaflets become thickened, distorted, and floppy over time. They no longer close properly, allowing blood to leak backwards into the left atrium with each heartbeat. This backward flow is called "regurgitation," and it produces the characteristic heart murmur that veterinarians detect with a stethoscope.

Over months to years, the regurgitation worsens. The left atrium enlarges to accommodate the extra blood volume. Eventually, the heart can't compensate any longer, and fluid begins to back up into the lungs — a condition called congestive heart failure. This is why early detection and proactive management are so important.

⚠️ Cavalier King Charles Spaniel Alert

If you have a Cavalier, this information is especially relevant for you. Studies show that approximately 50% of Cavaliers have a heart murmur by age 5, rising to nearly 100% by age 10. Annual heart examinations from age 2 are strongly recommended for this breed in Australia. The earlier MVD is detected, the more options you have.

Breeds at Risk in Australia

While MVD can affect any dog, certain breeds are genetically predisposed:

Very High Risk

Cavalier King Charles Spaniel — the breed most affected worldwide. Genetic testing and breeding programmes in Australia and abroad aim to reduce prevalence, but MVD remains extremely common. Early screening is essential.

High Risk

Small breeds generally — Chihuahuas, Toy Poodles, Pomeranians, Yorkshire Terriers, Maltese, Papillons, and Dachshunds all have elevated MVD rates. Risk increases significantly with age.

Moderate Risk

Medium breeds — Cocker Spaniels, Beagles, and Miniature Schnauzers have moderate predisposition. Large breeds can also develop MVD, though dilated cardiomyopathy is more common in these breeds.

🐕❤️
Cavalier King Charles Spaniels are beloved family dogs across Australia, but their genetic predisposition to MVD means regular heart checks should begin early — ideally from age 2 onwards.

Understanding the Stages of MVD

Veterinarians use a standardised staging system developed by the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM). Understanding these stages helps you know where your dog is in the disease process and what to expect:

🟢 Stage A
No murmur detected, but the dog is at risk due to breed or family history. No treatment needed, but regular monitoring is recommended.
Annual veterinary examinations recommended
🟢 Stage B1
Heart murmur present, but no heart enlargement on X-ray or echocardiogram. The body is compensating well. No medication needed yet.
Monitoring every 6–12 months with chest X-rays or echo
🟡 Stage B2
Heart murmur present with documented heart enlargement, but no symptoms. This is when the EPIC Study showed pimobendan can extend life.
Start pimobendan — can extend asymptomatic period by ~15 months
🔴 Stage C
Clinical signs of heart failure present (coughing, difficulty breathing, exercise intolerance). Requires multiple medications.
Pimobendan + diuretics + ACE inhibitors; intensive management
⚫ Stage D
Refractory heart failure — symptoms persist despite standard treatment. Requires more aggressive therapy.
Specialist referral; higher diuretic doses; consider palliative care

Medications Used to Treat MVD

Several medications are used to manage MVD in Australia. Your veterinarian will determine which combination is appropriate based on your dog's stage and individual circumstances:

Medication What It Does Monthly Cost (AUD)
Pimobendan (Vetmedin) Strengthens heart contractions and dilates blood vessels. The cornerstone of MVD treatment. Shown to extend life in stages B2 and C. $60–120
Furosemide (Frusemide) Diuretic that removes excess fluid from the body. Essential for congestive heart failure to prevent fluid in the lungs. $15–30
Benazepril (Fortekor) ACE inhibitor that dilates blood vessels and reduces workload on the heart. Often combined with pimobendan. $30–50
Spironolactone Potassium-sparing diuretic used alongside furosemide in advanced cases. Helps manage fluid retention. $25–45

Important: Never adjust your dog's medication without veterinary guidance. Dosages are carefully calculated based on your dog's weight, stage, and response to treatment. Regular blood tests may be needed to monitor kidney function and electrolyte levels, particularly with diuretics.

Home Monitoring: What You Can Do

As a dog owner, you play a crucial role in monitoring your dog's condition between veterinary visits. Here's what to watch for:

Resting Respiratory Rate

Count your dog's breaths when they're asleep or resting calmly. Count for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. A consistent resting rate under 30 breaths per minute is normal. Rates consistently above 30 may indicate fluid building up in the lungs — contact your vet.

Appetite and Energy

Note changes in appetite, enthusiasm for walks, or willingness to play. Decreasing energy levels can be an early warning sign that the heart is struggling. Trust your instincts — you know your dog best.

Coughing

A new or worsening cough, especially after exercise, excitement, or at night, can indicate heart enlargement pressing on the airways or fluid in the lungs. Report any coughing to your veterinarian.

Weight and Body Condition

Monitor your dog's weight. Unexplained weight loss can occur with advanced heart disease. Conversely, maintaining a healthy weight reduces cardiac workload — obesity makes the heart work harder.

Prognosis and Life Expectancy

One of the most common questions Australian pet owners ask is: "How long will my dog live with MVD?" The answer depends heavily on the stage at diagnosis and how well the disease is managed:

  • Stage B2 (enlargement, no symptoms) — With pimobendan started at this stage, dogs lived a median of 15 additional months before developing heart failure in the EPIC Study. Some dogs remain stable for years.
  • Stage C (heart failure) — With appropriate medication, most dogs live 6–18 months after developing congestive heart failure. Some live longer with careful management.
  • Stage D (refractory) — Weeks to months, depending on response to aggressive treatment. Quality of life becomes the primary consideration.

These are averages — individual dogs may do better or worse. What matters most is quality of life. With appropriate medication, many dogs with MVD continue to enjoy good days, gentle walks, and time with their families for months to years after diagnosis.
