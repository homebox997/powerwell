Senior Dog Arthritis: Early Signs, Daily Comfort and When to See a Vet

Arthritis is common in older dogs, but stiffness is not something your mate has to simply put up with. Learn the early changes worth watching, simple home comforts that help, and when a veterinary check is the right next step.

What Arthritis in Senior Dogs Really Means

Arthritis (“osteoarthritis”) is the gradual wearing of the cushioning cartilage inside a joint. As the cartilage thins, movement becomes stiffer and sometimes painful. It most often affects the hips, elbows, knees and spine of senior dogs.

Older dogs are more likely to develop it because joints have carried years of activity, and the cartilage renews more slowly with age. You may notice your dog slowing down after a rest, hesitating before a jump, or being less keen on the walk they used to love. These changes are easy to dismiss as “just getting old” — but they are worth noting, because early support can keep a dog comfortable for longer.

This page is educational. It does not diagnose your dog or replace a veterinary examination. If you are unsure about a change you have seen, a vet check is always the safe choice.

At-Home Symptom Watch: Early, Book-a-Vet, and Urgent

A home watch table helps you organise changes so you can describe them clearly to your vet. It is a recording aid only — it cannot confirm arthritis or replace an examination.

🟢 Early changes
Notice: slower to rise, brief stiffness after rest, less keen on long walks.
Record: time of day it appears, which joints, how long it lasts.
Next: record the change for a few days. Book sooner if it persists, worsens, appears painful or starts limiting normal activity.
🟡 Book a vet appointment
Notice: limping that does not settle, favouring a leg, reluctance to jump or climb.
Record: which leg, how often, any swelling or heat.
Next: book a check; your vet may suggest an exam and, where useful, X-rays.
🟠 Urgent warning signs
Notice: cries when touched, will not bear weight, joint swelling or heat, stops eating.
Record: when it started and how severe.
Next: contact your vet promptly or an emergency clinic.

Common Myths — and What’s Actually True

Myth: A slower older dog is just “normal ageing.”

More accurate: slowing down can be an early sign of joint discomfort worth watching. What to do: note when it happens and raise it at a routine vet visit rather than assuming nothing can help.

Myth: If my dog doesn’t cry out, it isn’t in pain.

More accurate: dogs often hide pain and simply move differently. What to do: watch for quieter changes — less play, more resting, hesitation on stairs.

Myth: An arthritic dog should stop exercising completely.

More accurate: gentle, regular movement usually helps keep joints mobile. What to do: short soft-surface walks, or controlled swimming or hydrotherapy when a vet or rehab professional recommends it, avoiding hard sprints and jumps.

Myth: Supplements can replace a veterinary check.

More accurate: supplements may support comfort but are not a diagnosis or treatment plan. What to do: have a vet assess the joints before starting anything new.

Myth: A new diet alone will fix arthritis.

More accurate: weight control and joint-support nutrition are helpful parts of care, not a cure. What to do: combine them with your vet’s plan and home adjustments.

Daily Care Tips You Can Start Today

Keep a healthy weight

Extra weight loads the joints. Even a small, vet-guided weight reduction can ease day-to-day discomfort. Ask your vet for a target suited to your dog’s breed and size.

Joint-support nutrition

Omega-3 (fish oil) has some supporting evidence for joint comfort. Glucosamine, chondroitin and green-lipped mussel are widely used, but the evidence is mixed. Ask your vet before starting any supplement.

A warm, supportive bed

Orthopaedic memory-foam bedding cushions joints and reduces pressure points. Keep it warm; cold can worsen morning stiffness. Place it somewhere easy to step onto without jumping.

Gentle, regular movement

Short daily walks on level, stable and non-slip surfaces help keep the muscles that support the joints. Controlled swimming or hydrotherapy may help some dogs when recommended by a vet or rehabilitation professional. Avoid sudden long hikes or hard running that overload sore joints.

Simple home modifications

Non-slip mats on tiles, a ramp for the car or couch, and a raised bowl reduce strain. A basic DIY ramp can make a real difference to daily comfort.

How a Vet May Assess the Problem

Arthritis is confirmed through a hands-on assessment, not guesswork at home. Depending on your dog’s condition, your vet may recommend one or more of the steps below.

Home monitoring you can do

Keep a simple note of when stiffness appears, which legs are affected, how far your dog walks comfortably, and any changes in appetite or mood. This helps your vet see the pattern.

Routine veterinary assessment

Your vet may examine how your dog moves and feels each joint, check for swelling or reduced range, and where useful suggest X-rays or basic blood tests. Based on what they find, your vet may recommend a tailored plan.

When urgent care may be needed

If your dog will not bear weight, a joint is hot or swollen, or they stop eating or seem in strong pain, contact your vet promptly. These are not changes to keep watching at home.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of arthritis in dogs?

Common early signs include stiffness after rest (especially mornings), slowing on walks, reluctance to jump or climb stairs, and favouring one leg. They are easy to miss, so note them over a week or two.

Can arthritis in dogs be cured?

Arthritis cannot be cured, but it can often be managed well. Weight control, joint-support nutrition, gentle exercise, home adjustments and a vet-guided plan can help your dog stay comfortable.

What supplements help dogs with arthritis?

Omega-3 from fish oil has some supporting evidence for joint comfort. Glucosamine, chondroitin and green-lipped mussel are commonly used, but studies show mixed results. Ask your vet before starting any supplement.

Which breeds are most prone to arthritis?

Larger breeds such as Labradors, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds and Rottweilers have higher risk, but any dog — including small seniors — can develop joint changes with age.

Is arthritis in dogs painful?

It can be. Dogs often hide pain, so watch for quieter changes: less play, more resting, hesitation on stairs, or mood shifts. If you suspect pain, a vet check is the safe step.

How can I help my arthritic dog at home?

Keep a healthy weight, use a warm orthopaedic bed, keep short gentle walks, add non-slip mats and ramps, and follow your vet’s plan. Small daily changes add up.

Should my dog still exercise with arthritis?

Usually yes — gentle, regular movement helps. Short walks on level, stable, non-slip surfaces are good, and controlled swimming or hydrotherapy may help some dogs when a vet or rehab professional recommends it. Hard running and jumping are not. Your vet can suggest a safe routine.

When should I take my dog to the vet for joint pain?

Book a check if limping does not settle, a leg is favoured, or your dog is reluctant to move. Seek prompt care for non-weight-bearing, swelling, heat or loss of appetite.
