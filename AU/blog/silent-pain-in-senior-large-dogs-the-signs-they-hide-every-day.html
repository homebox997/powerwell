Silent Pain in Senior Large Dogs: The Signs They Hide Every Day

August 11, 2026 · 6 min read · PawWell Editorial

Its a myth your old dog will yelp when it hurts

You might notice your big old Labrador stumbles on the back steps or takes longer to get up from his bed, but still wags his tail and never whimpers. So you assume hes just slowing down with agenot that hes in pain. This is one of the most common misunderstandings I see as a vet. Dogs are masters at hiding discomfort. By the time they cry out, the issue has often been building silently for months.

What this guide helps you do

This article will help you spot the secret signs of pain in a senior large-breed dog, using simple at-home checks you can do tonight. Its not a diagnosisyour vet is the only person who can confirm whats going onbut it can give you the words to describe what youre seeing so your vet gets the full picture on the first visit.

One small story: Banjo the Golden Retriever

Illustrative scenario not a real patient. Banjo is a nine-year-old Golden Retriever from Newcastle. His family thought he was just getting lazier. Hed flop onto his side after a short walk and stop chasing the footy. But because he never limped or made a sound, they believed he wasnt sore. A vet assessment later found mild to moderate elbow dysplasia and early hip arthritis. Banjo wasnt lazy; he was protecting himself from constant, dull pain. With a gentle pain management plan, he began trotting up the hallway again within weeks.

The hidden language of pain in big dogs

Large breeds like Labradors, Rottweilers, and Bernese Mountain Dogs are particularly stoic. Biologically, showing weakness makes a social animal vulnerable, so evolution has taught them to hide it. Instead of crying, a dog in pain may show:

  • Posture shifts: standing with an arched back or tucking the belly tightly.
  • Weight shifts: putting more weight on the front legs even when standing still.
  • Subtle movement changes: bunny-hopping up the stairs, using the front legs to pull the body up, or sliding sideways off the couch instead of jumping.
  • Resting changes: lying in one spot all day, groaning softly when settling down, or repeatedly licking a joint (the fur may even look pink from saliva).
  • Behaviour changes: becoming grumpy when touched near the hips or shoulders, avoiding pats, or seeming depressed and uninterested in food or short walkies.

None of these signs involve a yelp, yet every single one can be a quiet plea for help.

Try this tonight: an at-home pain checklist you can do now

Set aside ten quiet minutes. Youre not diagnosingyoure observing like a reporter. Grab your phone to jot a few notes.

  1. Watch the sit and rise. Call your dog over and ask for a sit. Then ask them to lie down, then get up again. Does the movement look fluid, or is it a slow-motion task with a pause partway? A dog that plops the back end down suddenly may be protecting painful hips.
  2. Notice stairs and steps. If you have a few steps outside or inside, stand nearby without coaxing and see how your dog navigates them. Senior dogs with joint pain often step up with just the forelimbs and drag the hind end up last, or they avoid stairs altogether.
  3. Check favourite spots. Dogs in pain often choose softer, warmer surfaces and avoid hard tiles or floorboards. If your dog suddenly refuses the kitchen floor they used to lie on, thats worth noting.
  4. Gently run your hands along the spine and legs. Do not press hard. Just use flat palms. A twitch away, a quick turn of the head, or a hard, tense muscle may signal soreness underneath. Pay extra attention to the lower back and hipscommon trouble spots for large breeds.
  5. Assess the wag and interest scale. Does your dog still perk up at the lead, or do they hide when you rattle it? A gradual loss of enthusiasm for walks or a flat, low tail carriage can point to persistent, low-grade pain.

If you tick two or more of these, it doesnt mean your dog is definitely hurting, but its a strong enough flag to book a senior health check and pain assessment with your vet.

When to call your vet right away

Most senior pain can be managed with a calm, team approach. However, some situations need urgent attention. If you see any of the following, dont wait until morningcall your vet or the nearest emergency hospital immediately:

  • Your dog cannot put any weight on a leg.
  • There is obvious swelling or deformity after an injury.
  • Your dog shows sudden severe painvocalising, flinching, hiding, or seeming unable to stand at all.

Never give human pain medication like ibuprofen, paracetamol, or aspirin. These can be fatally toxic to dogs and cause irreversible organ damage.

What this might mean for your big dogs future

Learning that your quiet senior has been living with pain can feel like a gut-punch. But heres the hopeful bit: recognising those subtle signs is the single most powerful step you can take. With early identification, large-breed dogs can benefit enormously from weight management, joint support diets, gentle physio, and safe pain relief tailored by your vet. Even simple changesa ramp for the back steps, non-slip mats, a supportive orthopaedic bedcan turn a hard day into a soft one.

Pain that doesnt shout still deserves to be heard. Youre not imagining things if your dog just seems a bit off. Trust your gut and know that asking a vet, Could he be sore? is never a waste of time. Its the question that often changes everything.

Frequently Asked Questions

If my old dog is still eating and wagging his tail, can he really be in pain? Absolutely. Appetite and tail wags are survival instincts, not pain-proof evidence. Many dogs maintain a front of normality even with significant chronic pain. The subtle signslike stiffness after rest or reduced playare often the real clues.

My big dog sometimes limps for a step or two but then walks normally. Should I still see the vet? Yes. An intermittent starter stiffness or occasional limp is a classic marker of osteoarthritis in large breeds. Early management can slow progression and keep your dog comfortable much longer.

Are there any supplements I can start at home to help? Some large-breed owners find omega-3 fatty acids or glucosamine-based supplements helpful, but you must speak with your vet first. The dose, quality, and interactions with other medications all need professional oversightnever self-prescribe.
