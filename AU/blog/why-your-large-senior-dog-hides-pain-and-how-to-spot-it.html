Joint Care

Why Your Large Senior Dog Hides Pain (and How to Spot It)

August 08, 2026 · 6 min read · PawWell Editorial

Hes not crying, so he must be fineand other myths

Brooklyn is an eight-year-old Labrador who used to bound up the back steps. Lately, shes been getting up a little slower after her afternoon kip on the deck. Her owner, Dave, tells me, She never yelps or whines, so I didnt think she was actually hurting. That single belief stops thousands of big-dog families from seeing whats really going on. Dogs descended from wild canids, and a noisy pack member didnt survive long. So your gentle giant has kept an ancient rule: never cry out unless the pain is overwhelming. By the time a large-breed dog does vocalise, the discomfort is usually significant. The good news is that once you know the quiet signs, you can act early. In this guide, Ill walk you through what silent pain looks like in a senior Labrador, Golden Retriever, German Shepherd, Rottweiler, Bernese, or Mastiffand what you can do tonight to help.

The short answer for the dog mum or dad on the run

Yes, your dog can be in pain right now without making a sound. Large breeds are especially good at masking discomfort because their deep chests, stoic temperaments, and working-dog heritage push them to tough it out. Watch for changes in daily habitsa slower sit, a shorter walk, a reluctance to jump into the uterather than waiting for a yelp. If you notice any of these, a vet check is your next step. This article will help you separate normal ageing from pain that needs attention.

An everyday story (illustrative, not a real patient)

Illustrative scenario not a real patient. Bear, a nine-year-old Rottweiler from Geelong, still wagged his stump and licked his owners hand every morning. But his family noticed hed stopped climbing the stairs to the kids bedroom and would circle his mat for a full minute before lying down. No limp, no whimper. The vet found early elbow arthritis and muscle tension across his shoulders from compensating. With gentle pain management, environmental tweaks, and short leash walks, Bear was back to his old self within weeksbut only because someone read the silent signals.

The survival instinct that keeps big dogs quiet

For thousands of years, a wild canine that showed weakness risked being cast out or picked off by predators. Large-bodied dogs carry that instinct deep in their genetic memory. Even our beloved couch-hogging Labradors will hide a limp until the pain interferes with a high-value reward like the morning walk. This is not stubbornness; its a hard-wired survival trait. And because big breeds carry more weight on their skeletons, the subtle signs of discomforta slightly shorter stride, a head held lower, a reluctance to turn sharply on pavementcan be the only clue you get.

Your dog isnt being tough to confuse you. Hes just being a dog. The shift in your thinking is to stop asking Does it hurt? and to start asking Has anything changed? Thats the lens that protects your mates quality of life.

Tonights at-home pain check (your self-check checklist)

You dont need fancy equipment. Spend ten quiet minutes observing your dog move through ordinary moments. Check these five things tonight:

  • The sit-to-stand transition: Does your dog hesitate, rock forwards a few times, or use front legs to pull themselves up? A smooth, single-motion rise is the normal for a large dog.
  • The evening stretch: Notice whether your dog still arches into a deep play bow stretch after a nap. Shallow, hurried stretches can signal stiffness in the spine, hips, or knees.
  • Turning on a leash: On flat ground, guide your dog in a figure-of-eight at walking pace. A dog avoiding hip or elbow rotation will often swing their whole body instead of bending comfortably at the joints.
  • Response to gentle touch: Run your fingertips lightly along the spine from the neck to the base of the tail, then down each leg. A sudden twitch, skin flicker, or quick turn of the head can point to a sore spoteven if the dog doesnt pull away.
  • Resting breathing pattern: When your dog is truly relaxed, count the breaths over 30 seconds. A rate above 25-30 breaths per minute at complete rest, especially with shallow panting, can sometimes suggest underlying discomfortthough it can also come from heat or anxiety, so look for a pattern over several nights.

Dont turn this into an exam. Just watch, and note anything different. The checklist gives you solid information to share with your vet, not a diagnosis.

When to call your vet right away

If your dog cannot put any weight on a leg, has obvious swelling or deformity after an injury, shows sudden severe pain (vocalising, flinching, hiding), or seems unable to stand at allthis needs urgent vet assessment. Do not give human pain medication.

Beyond that, book a vet appointment soon if you notice any of the following more-than-normal changes:

  • Refusing to go for a walk when they normally charge out the door.
  • Whining or trembling at rest, especially at night.
  • Repeatedly licking or chewing a specific joint or paw.
  • Sudden change in appetite or water intake with a mobility change.
  • Any unexplained withdrawalstaying in another room, not greeting you at the door.

These dogs dont use words, but those changes are their voice. Listening early usually means simpler, more effective care.

Small daily changes that make a big difference

While youre waiting for the vet appointment or acting on their plan, you can soften your large dogs daily world:

  • Slip-resistant mats on floorboards and tiles give weary joints more confidence. A single mat by the back door is often the one that helps most.
  • Raised food and water bowls at elbow height take pressure off the neck and shoulders, especially for deep-chested breeds like the Rottweiler or Mastiff.
  • Shorter, sniff-led walks. Swap the long beach session for two 10-minute meanders with plenty of time to sniff. The mental richness calms the nervous system, and the gentle movement lubricates stiff joints without overloading them.
  • Non-slip ramp or a helping towel. A sturdy ramp into the back of the ute, or a towel slung under the belly to support the back end, can preserve your dogs dignity and keep them included in family outings.

If youre thinking about joint supplements or weight management support, have that conversation with your vet. Even gentle exercise adjustments work best when theyre tailored to your dogs specific needs. You can also learn more about how extra weight affects big-dog joints in our arthritis care guide.

Frequently Asked Questions

My old Labrador still wags his tail. Can he really be in pain?
Absolutely. Tail wagging is social communication, not a pain report. A dog can wag at the door while standing with a sore back and then collapse in bed once youre gone. Think of it like a person smiling at a mate while dealing with a headachethe social instinct overrides the discomfort for a short burst.

Do German Shepherds and Rottweilers feel pain differently?
No breed feels less pain, but some large working breeds are more tolerant of low-grade, ongoing achesmeaning they can live with stiff hips for months before limping. The nervous system processes the signal just the same; theyre just quieter messengers.

Is it safe to give my dog a human pain tablet in a smaller dose?
No. Human pain relievers, even in tiny amounts, can cause fatal stomach ulcers, kidney failure, or liver damage in dogs. Only veterinarian-prescribed medications are safe. This is an absolute red linenever share your medicine cabinet with your dog.

A gentle nudge from someone who gets it

Noticing these quiet changes can feel heavy. It makes you wonder if you missed something, or if you should have acted sooner. Please know that most of us have been there, and youre noticing nowwhich is exactly when care can still do the most good. Your big old mate doesnt need you to feel guilty; he needs you to see him clearly. With some observation tonight, a vet check when youre ready, and the gentle adjustments weve talked about, youre already changing his comfort world for the better. Thats real love for a large senior dog, right where the Australian sun touches the verandah.
