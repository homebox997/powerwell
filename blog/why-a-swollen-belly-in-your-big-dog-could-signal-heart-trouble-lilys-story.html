Why a Swollen Belly in Your Big Dog Could Signal Heart Trouble: Lily’s Story

心脏护理 · Published July 26, 2026 · 4 min read

---

Picture this: You're enjoying a lazy Sunday arvo in your Perth backyard, when you notice your usually playful Bullmastiff, Lily, is looking a bit off. Her belly seems rounder than usual, and she's panting even though it's not that hot. Over the next day or two, the swelling gets worse and she barely touches her dinner. If this sounds familiar, you’re not alone—and it could be a sign of something serious going on with her heart.

Meet Lily, a nine-year-old Bullmastiff whose story taught her family (and us) just how sneaky heart problems can be in our gentle giants. Her symptoms—rapid belly swelling, breathing troubles, tiredness, and a strange fluidy feel when you pressed her tummy—were all red flags for right-sided congestive heart failure.

Why Was Lily’s Belly Swelling? The Heart Connection

When the right side of a dog’s heart can’t pump blood forward efficiently, fluid can back up and leak into the belly, causing it to swell like a water balloon. This is what happened to Lily. Her heart was racing at over 220 beats per minute, but when her vet felt the pulse in her back leg, they could only find 70 weak beats. That huge gap is a classic sign of a chaotic heart rhythm called atrial fibrillation—the heart was quivering rather than squeezing, so very little blood was getting pushed around her body.

Try this at home: Gently feel your dog’s inner thigh for the femoral pulse while you listen or feel their heartbeat on the chest. If the pulse rate is a lot lower than the heart rate, or the rhythm feels all over the shop, it’s time to see the vet straight away.

A Quiet Heart That Still Needed Help

You might think a serious heart condition always comes with a loud, obvious murmur. Not necessarily. Lily had only the faintest murmur, yet her heart was failing. Many big dogs with weakened heart muscle (a condition called dilated cardiomyopathy) can have a "silent" heart, so a quiet stethoscope check doesn’t rule out trouble. X-rays showed Lily’s heart was massively enlarged, and an ultrasound revealed the upper left chamber was bigger than normal, even though the valves weren’t the main problem. And in an unexpected twist, the scan also found a small, slow-growing tumour at the base of her heart. This discovery highlighted why a full diagnostic workup—ECG, X-ray, and ultrasound—matters, especially in giant breeds.

Emergency Help and Long-Term Care for Lily

At the vet hospital, the team gave Lily oxygen, a strong diuretic to flush out the extra belly fluid, a medicine to strengthen her heart’s squeeze, and a gentle sedative to help her breathe calmly. Within hours, she was much more comfortable. Once stable, she started a daily tablet routine: one to keep her heart rhythm under control, another to protect the heart muscle and blood vessels, and a mild fluid pill to prevent fluid building up again. Thanks to this mix, Lily went home breathing easy and was soon back to enjoying short walks and belly rubs. Her family now keeps a close eye on her tummy and energy levels, and they know to act fast if anything changes.

What Every Big Dog Owner Can Do Tonight

1. Learn the emergency signs

  • Belly changes: Looks bigger, feels tight or sloshy like a water balloon.
  • Breathing: Faster or harder at rest—count breaths while your dog sleeps; more than 30 breaths per minute is a red flag.
  • Coughing: Especially at night or after lying down.
  • Sudden tiredness: Not keen to walk to the letterbox or chase a ball.

2. Check your dog’s pulse at home

Find the femoral artery inside the back leg, near the groin. Compare the beat you feel there with the heartbeat on the chest. If the beats don’t match up, or the rhythm is chaotic, don’t wait—call your vet.

3. Book a yearly heart check for your giant breed

If your dog is a Bullmastiff, Mastiff, Great Dane, Irish Wolfhound, or similar large breed, start annual heart screenings from age 4–5. A simple vet listen, an ECG (heart trace), and possibly an ultrasound can catch early rhythm changes long before they turn into an emergency. Early treatment can add years of happy zoomies.

4. Don’t rely on a "quiet" stethoscope check

No murmur doesn’t mean no heart disease. If your big dog shows any of the signs above, ask your vet about imaging, even if the heart sounds okay. Trust your gut and be the voice for your mate.

The Bottom Line

Lily’s story reminds us that a swollen belly isn’t always just a bit of extra pudding. In big dogs, it can be the first whisper of a struggling heart. With quick vet care and the right daily medicine, Lily bounced back to enjoy her walks and treats. If your mate ever shows these signs, don’t wait—the best thing you can do is trust your gut and get them checked. Because our big, slobbery friends rely on us to listen when their hearts are trying to tell us something.
