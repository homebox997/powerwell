Senior Dog Kidney Health

Senior Dog Kidney Ageing: Normal Change or Kidney Disease?

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 9 August 2026

Quick answer: Kidneys can become more vulnerable as a dog grows older, but “senior” is not a kidney diagnosis. Drinking more, urinating more, losing weight or eating less may occur with kidney disease, yet each has several other possible causes. Veterinarians separate normal variation from disease by looking for persistent changes and combining the history, examination, blood results, urinalysis, blood pressure and sometimes imaging—not by relying on age or one number.

You refill the water bowl before bed and wonder whether you simply forgot doing it that morning. A week later, your dog asks to go outside at 3 am. Nothing else seems dramatic. The appetite is mostly there, the tail still moves at breakfast, and it feels unfair to turn one extra drink into a medical worry.

That uncertainty is normal. Water intake changes with weather, food, activity and medication. Large dogs also empty a bowl faster because they need more water than small dogs. What matters is a genuine change from your dog’s usual pattern—especially when it persists or arrives with changes in urine, weight, appetite, vomiting or energy.

Seek urgent veterinary care if your dog cannot pass urine, repeatedly vomits, becomes severely weak, collapses, has a seizure, may have swallowed a toxin, refuses food and water with other concerning signs, or deteriorates quickly. Do not wait for a routine blood test and do not try to force large amounts of water.

Age changes risk, not the rules of diagnosis

Kidneys filter waste from the blood, help balance water and minerals, support blood-pressure control and contribute to red-blood-cell production. They have considerable reserve, so a dog can appear well while early changes are developing. This is one reason routine senior checks can be useful even when the household has not noticed a clear symptom.

Chronic kidney disease, or CKD, means kidney abnormalities have persisted over time. It is different from acute kidney injury, which develops suddenly and may follow toxin exposure, severe dehydration, infection, poor blood flow, obstruction or other events. A dog can also have an acute problem on top of chronic disease. The distinction affects testing, treatment and what changes might be reversible.

IRIS—the International Renal Interest Society—emphasises that CKD staging is used only after CKD has been diagnosed and the patient is stable. A rapidly changing creatinine or SDMA result should not be treated as a stable CKD stage. This protects owners and clinicians from turning one abnormal day into a permanent label.

Changes that can look like “ordinary old age”

Kidney disease often begins with non-specific clues. Non-specific means the sign is real, but it does not point to one organ by itself.

This overlap explains why a symptom checklist can guide an appointment but cannot diagnose CKD. It also explains why it is unhelpful to say, “All old dogs drink more.” A repeated increase deserves investigation even though the final cause may not be kidney disease.

The difference between one result and a kidney trend

Creatinine

Creatinine is a waste product used as an indirect marker of kidney filtration. It is familiar and useful, but it is affected by more than kidney function. Dehydration can increase it. Low muscle mass can make it appear lower than expected, which matters in an older dog who has lost muscle over the spine and thighs. A blocked urinary tract can also alter kidney-related values.

SDMA

SDMA is another blood marker used in kidney assessment. It is generally less influenced by muscle mass than creatinine, but it is not a stand-alone home truth. IRIS guidance uses persistent results and the full clinical picture. When values disagree or the patient is unstable, repeat assessment may be needed.

Urea or BUN

Urea can rise with reduced kidney filtration, but diet, dehydration, gastrointestinal bleeding and other factors can influence it. This is why a panel is interpreted as a pattern rather than as a row of isolated red flags.

Urinalysis

Urine supplies information that blood cannot. A urinalysis may examine concentration, protein, glucose, blood, cells, crystals, bacteria and sediment. Poorly concentrated urine can support concern about kidney function in the right context, but concentration also changes with water intake, medication and other diseases. A urine culture may be needed when infection is suspected.

Blood pressure and urine protein

IRIS recommends substaging diagnosed CKD by assessing urine protein and systemic blood pressure. These findings matter because protein loss and hypertension can influence risk and management. Neither can be judged from the way a dog behaves at home.

Why repeat testing matters: A stable result over time and a sudden change tell different stories. IRIS notes that longitudinal assessment—following results across time—usually provides better information than a one-off value. Whenever possible, compare the same dog, not your dog with a number posted online.

What “normal for age” really means

A reference interval tells a clinician how a result compares with a population. It does not automatically prove that a changing result is ideal for your particular dog. A value that remains inside the laboratory range but steadily moves away from a long-standing baseline may deserve a closer look. Equally, a mild abnormality during dehydration may improve once the immediate problem is corrected.

Healthy ageing should not be defined as ignoring gradual decline. It is better understood as maintaining comfort and function without evidence of untreated disease. An older dog may sleep more or prefer shorter outings while keeping a stable appetite, body condition, hydration and toilet routine. A new pattern of thirst, urine changes or weight loss is not made normal simply because it arrived slowly.

Large body size adds another practical challenge: measuring water by eye is unreliable. One Labrador can make a bowl look dramatically emptier than a small terrier. Multiple pets, outdoor bowls, spilled water and wet food can all confuse the estimate. That is a reason to measure more carefully, not a reason to guess.

A practical three-day water and urine record

If your dog is comfortable and has free access to water, a short log can help. Do not restrict water to make the measurement easier, and do not delay an appointment to finish the record.

  1. Measure water offered. Use the same jug to record how much goes into every bowl.
  2. Measure what remains. Note refills, spills and water shared with other animals. The result is an estimate, not a laboratory test.
  3. Record food type. Wet food contributes water; a switch from dry to wet food can change drinking.
  4. Count toilet requests. Note overnight trips, accidents and whether the volume appears larger or smaller than usual.
  5. Watch the act of urinating. Straining, repeated attempts, discomfort, blood or inability to pass urine requires prompt advice.
  6. Add context. Record hot weather, unusual exercise, vomiting and every medication or supplement.

A veterinarian can tell you whether a measured intake is concerning for your dog’s size and situation. Online cut-offs are easily misused when water in food, heat, medication and measurement error are ignored.

What a veterinarian may do at the first kidney-focused visit

The appointment often begins with details that seem ordinary: appetite, diet, water, toilet routine, weight change, vomiting, medications, toxin access and previous illnesses. The physical examination may assess hydration, mouth, body and muscle condition, abdomen, heart and circulation, and signs of pain or systemic illness.

Blood chemistry and a complete blood count can look at kidney-related values, electrolytes, red blood cells and other organs. Urinalysis is commonly paired with bloodwork because each gives context to the other. Blood pressure, a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, urine culture, X-rays or ultrasound may be recommended depending on the initial findings.

Imaging can examine kidney size and shape and look for stones, obstruction or structural change, but appearance alone does not measure all kidney function. Likewise, bloodwork alone cannot describe every urinary problem. The aim is to build one coherent picture.

Common mistakes that make early kidney changes harder to see

  • Restricting water. Increased thirst is a clue to investigate, not a behaviour to punish or suppress.
  • Waiting for urine colour to change. Kidney disease can be present without a dramatic visible colour change.
  • Assuming weight loss is healthy. Loss of muscle in a senior dog is not the same as safe loss of excess fat.
  • Reading one value without hydration or muscle context. Numbers need the patient attached to them.
  • Starting a kidney diet or supplement before diagnosis. Nutritional needs vary by disease, stage, body condition and other health problems.
  • Using human painkillers. Ibuprofen, naproxen and other human medicines can be dangerous for dogs and may injure the kidneys or gastrointestinal tract.

Questions that make a kidney discussion clearer

  • Is my dog stable and hydrated enough for these results to be interpreted?
  • Do the blood and urine findings agree?
  • Could muscle loss be affecting creatinine?
  • Is there persistent evidence of CKD, or do we need repeat testing first?
  • Were urine protein and blood pressure assessed?
  • Could infection, obstruction, medication or another disease explain the change?
  • Which home changes should trigger a same-day call?

If CKD is diagnosed, ask what the stage and substage mean for the current plan rather than using the label to predict an exact future. IRIS stages help organise management; they do not tell the entire story of comfort, appetite, concurrent disease or response to care.

A calm plan for a dog whose tests are currently normal

Normal results are good news, but they are most useful as a baseline. Keep copies. At future checks, the veterinarian can compare weight, muscle condition, creatinine, SDMA where used, urine concentration, protein and blood pressure. The right recheck interval depends on age, breed, previous illness and medication.

Continue offering fresh water at all times. Feed a complete, balanced diet suited to the individual dog unless your veterinarian recommends otherwise. Do not place a healthy dog on a restrictive kidney diet simply because they are old. Tell the clinic about over-the-counter products as well as prescriptions, because “natural” does not mean irrelevant to kidney health.

Why body condition and muscle condition belong in the kidney record

The scale cannot tell you whether a dog has lost fat, muscle or both. A large senior dog may weigh almost the same while the thighs and muscles along the spine become smaller. Another may lose excess fat safely while maintaining good muscle. These are different stories, and kidney blood markers are easier to interpret when the veterinarian knows which story applies.

Ask the clinic to explain both body condition and muscle condition. At home, take a monthly photo from the side and above on the same non-slip surface. Run your hands gently over the ribs, spine, shoulders and thighs without pressing painful areas. Note a loosening collar or harness, a more prominent spine, reduced thigh shape or difficulty completing familiar movements.

Do not respond to unexplained weight loss by constantly changing foods or adding high-protein treats before an assessment. The immediate priority is to find out whether reduced intake, nausea, dental pain, digestive disease, kidney disease or another condition is involved. Once the cause and nutritional needs are clearer, the veterinarian can help protect calories and muscle without creating a one-size-fits-all “senior kidney” diet.

Weighing frequency should suit the situation. Monthly checks may be enough for a stable dog; an unwell dog may need closer veterinary monitoring. Bathroom scales are awkward and unsafe for many large breeds, so ask whether the clinic permits quick weight checks between appointments. Trend the same dog on the same scale where practical.

Keep this in mind: Age is a reason to watch more carefully, not a diagnosis and not an excuse. The strongest kidney picture comes from persistent evidence: what you see at home, what the veterinarian finds on examination, and how blood, urine, pressure and imaging results fit together over time.

Frequently asked questions

Do dogs naturally develop kidney disease when they get old?

No. Age increases the value of screening but does not itself diagnose CKD. Diagnosis relies on persistent clinical or laboratory evidence interpreted in a stable patient after other causes are considered.

Does one high creatinine result mean chronic kidney disease?

Not necessarily. Hydration, muscle mass, urinary obstruction and other factors can affect results. Veterinarians interpret creatinine with the history, examination, urine findings, SDMA where appropriate and repeat testing.

Can a senior dog have kidney disease with normal-looking urine?

Yes. Owners cannot judge kidney function from colour or appearance alone. Urinalysis can assess concentration, protein, sediment and other features that are not visible at home.

Should I limit water if my senior dog is drinking more?

No. Keep fresh water available unless a veterinarian gives a specific instruction for an unusual clinical situation. Increased thirst needs investigation rather than water restriction.

When are kidney or urinary signs an emergency?

Seek urgent care for inability to urinate, repeated vomiting, severe weakness, collapse, seizures, suspected toxin exposure, rapid deterioration, or refusal of food and water with other concerning signs.
