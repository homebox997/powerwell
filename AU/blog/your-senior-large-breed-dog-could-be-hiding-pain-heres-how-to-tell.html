Could Your Senior Large-Breed Dog Be in Pain? Subtle Signs Owners Often Miss

August 01, 2026 · AgedPawWell

Your senior dog may not cry, whimper or develop an obvious limp when something hurts. Chronic pain often appears as a collection of small changes: taking longer to stand, walking more slowly, avoiding the car or becoming unusually restless at night.

Because these changes can develop gradually, they are easy to mistake for normal ageing. You may find yourself saying, "He's just slowing down," without realising that your dog has quietly adjusted his routine to avoid discomfort.

Ageing itself is not a disease, and persistent pain should not be accepted as an unavoidable part of getting older. Learning to notice changes in movement, behaviour and daily habits can help your veterinarian identify a problem earlier.

Why Chronic Pain Can Be Difficult to Recognise

Dogs express pain in different ways. Some vocalise or limp, while others simply stop doing activities that have become uncomfortable.

A dog with slowly developing joint pain has time to adapt. He may shift more weight onto another leg, choose a different sleeping position or wait for help before climbing into the car. These adjustments can make the problem less obvious, particularly when family members see the dog every day.

Large and giant breeds can also be difficult to assess because their size makes everyday assistance challenging. An owner may begin lifting the dog's back end, shortening walks or avoiding stairs without recognising that these changes have become part of a pain-management routine.

Pain is not always caused by arthritis. Dental disease, ear infections, abdominal illness, cancer, nerve problems and many other conditions can change a dog's behaviour. Observation provides useful clues, but a veterinary examination is needed to determine the cause.

Common Causes of Pain in Senior Large Dogs

Osteoarthritis is one of the most common causes of persistent pain and reduced mobility in older dogs. It is a complex joint disease involving inflammation and changes to cartilage, bone and surrounding tissues—not simply "wear and tear."

Large breeds may also experience pain associated with:

  • Hip or elbow dysplasia
  • Cranial cruciate ligament disease
  • Previous fractures or joint injuries
  • Spinal or nerve disease
  • Muscle and tendon injuries
  • Paw, nail or skin problems
  • Dental and oral disease
  • Abdominal or internal disease
  • Bone or soft-tissue tumours

The location and cause matter because each condition requires a different treatment plan. A supplement intended for joint support will not treat a torn ligament, infected tooth, spinal problem or bone tumour.

Changes in Movement

A dramatic limp is easy to notice. Earlier signs may be much quieter.

Taking longer to stand

Your dog may rock forwards several times, brace with the front legs or pause before pushing up. He may also lower himself awkwardly or drop the final few centimetres when lying down.

One difficult rise does not prove that your dog is in pain. Slippery flooring, weakness, fatigue and neurological problems can look similar. Repeated difficulty is worth recording and discussing with your veterinarian.

Stiffness after rest

Dogs with osteoarthritis may appear stiff after sleeping and move more freely after a few minutes of gentle activity. However, this pattern is not unique to arthritis.

New stiffness, worsening stiffness or a change affecting only one limb should not automatically be blamed on age.

Avoiding familiar activities

Watch for hesitation before:

  • Climbing stairs
  • Jumping into the car
  • Getting onto a bed or sofa
  • Walking across a slippery floor
  • Squatting to toilet
  • Lowering the head to eat
  • Turning around in a confined space

A dog may still complete the activity when encouraged, but reluctance can be an early sign that it has become uncomfortable.

A change in walking style

Shorter steps, reduced speed, a swaying hind end, scuffed nails or uneven wear on the paws may signal discomfort or weakness. Some dogs shift weight forwards to reduce pressure on painful hips or knees.

A bunny-hopping gait can occur with hip discomfort, but it does not identify the cause by itself. Likewise, a head bob can be associated with forelimb lameness, but interpreting gait correctly can be difficult without veterinary training.

Record a video rather than trying to diagnose which joint hurts.

Changes Beyond Walking

Pain can affect much more than movement.

Restlessness at night

A dog who repeatedly changes position, gets up and lies down, pants in a cool room or struggles to settle may be uncomfortable.

Night-time panting can also occur with anxiety, breathing problems, medication effects and other illnesses. If it is new or persistent, arrange a veterinary assessment.

Changes in social behaviour

A normally affectionate dog may withdraw, avoid being touched or leave when children or other pets approach. Another dog may become unusually clingy and seek reassurance.

Pain can also reduce tolerance. Growling, flinching or snapping when approached may be a warning that the dog feels vulnerable or uncomfortable. Do not punish the warning. Give your dog space and seek professional advice.

Licking one area repeatedly

Persistent licking or chewing of a paw, leg or joint may be associated with pain, but allergies, infection, wounds, foreign bodies and behavioural conditions are also possible.

Check the area visually if your dog allows it, but avoid repeatedly pressing or squeezing a painful spot.

Reduced interest in normal activities

Your dog may still eat and greet you while quietly giving up longer walks, play, exploring the garden or following the family between rooms.

Think about what your dog has stopped doing—not only what he is still able to do.

Appetite and toileting changes

Pain can reduce appetite or make it difficult to reach a food bowl. Back, hip or knee discomfort may also make it harder to posture for urination or defecation.

Loss of appetite, vomiting, a tense abdomen or difficulty toileting may indicate something other than joint pain and should be assessed promptly.

A Safe Home Observation Check

The aim is to collect useful information, not to provoke pain or perform a home orthopaedic examination.

Record everyday movement

Take short videos of your dog:

  • Rising from bed
  • Walking away from and towards the camera
  • Turning naturally
  • Using a familiar step, if safe
  • Moving before and after a normal gentle walk

Use a non-slip surface. Do not force your dog to trot, climb stairs, jump or repeat an action that appears painful.

Compare good and difficult days

Keep a simple weekly record of:

  • Ease of standing
  • Interest in walks
  • Ability to use stairs
  • Night-time restlessness
  • Appetite
  • Toileting posture
  • Interaction with family
  • Any limping or slipping

A short video diary can reveal gradual changes that are difficult to notice from memory alone.

Look without manipulating

Check the paws and nails for cracks, swelling, wounds or foreign material. Notice visible swelling or muscle loss.

If your dog is comfortable being touched, you may gently stroke over the body as you normally would. Stop if the dog tenses, turns the head sharply, moves away, growls or appears worried.

Do not bend joints through their range of motion, press firmly along the spine or attempt to reproduce the pain. A frightened or painful dog may bite even if he has never done so before.

When to Contact Your Veterinarian Promptly

Arrange an appointment if you notice:

  • Repeated difficulty rising or lying down
  • A new or persistent limp
  • Reduced interest in walks or play
  • Reluctance to climb stairs or enter the car
  • Night-time restlessness or unexplained panting
  • Repeated licking of one area
  • A noticeable change in temperament
  • Loss of muscle, reduced stamina or ongoing stiffness
  • Pain that appears to be worsening

Contact a veterinarian urgently if your dog:

  • Cannot stand or suddenly loses the use of a limb
  • Cannot put any weight on a leg
  • Cries out or shows severe, sudden pain
  • Has a swollen, hot or visibly deformed limb
  • Develops weakness, wobbliness or drags a paw
  • Has a painful or swollen abdomen
  • Stops eating and appears unwell
  • Has pale gums, breathing difficulty or collapses
  • Develops pain after a fall, collision or other trauma

New severe pain should not be managed at home with leftover medication.

How Chronic Pain May Be Managed

Effective pain management is usually tailored to the individual dog and may combine several approaches.

Depending on the diagnosis, a veterinary plan may include:

  • Prescription pain relief or anti-inflammatory medication
  • Weight optimisation
  • Controlled, appropriate exercise
  • Physical rehabilitation
  • Changes to flooring, stairs and sleeping areas
  • Ramps or supportive mobility equipment
  • Diet changes or selected supplements
  • Treatment of the underlying joint, spinal, dental or internal condition
  • Regular reassessment of comfort and function

Not every treatment is suitable for every senior dog. Kidney, liver, heart and gastrointestinal conditions can influence medication choices. Supplements may also interact with medications or vary in quality and evidence.

Never give ibuprofen, naproxen or other human pain medication unless a veterinarian has specifically prescribed a product and dose for your dog.

Movement Still Matters—but It Must Be Appropriate

For many dogs with osteoarthritis, mild to moderate regular exercise helps maintain muscle, mobility and confidence. Complete inactivity can contribute to weakness and make movement harder.

That does not mean pushing through pain. Long weekend walks, repeated ball chasing, slippery surfaces and sudden bursts of strenuous exercise may worsen symptoms.

The right amount depends on the diagnosis and current level of function. Shorter, more consistent walks may suit some dogs, while others need a period of restricted activity. Your veterinarian or qualified canine rehabilitation professional can help develop an appropriate plan.

Small Changes That Can Make Home Easier

While waiting for veterinary advice, practical adjustments may improve safety and comfort:

  • Place non-slip mats on smooth floors.
  • Keep food, water and bedding easy to reach.
  • Provide a supportive bed in a warm, quiet area.
  • Use a ramp only if it is stable, gently sloped and your dog can use it safely.
  • Block access to stairs if slipping is a concern.
  • Keep nails and paw fur appropriately trimmed.
  • Use a well-fitted harness for controlled support when recommended.

Avoid lifting a large dog without knowing the safest technique. Incorrect lifting can hurt both you and your dog.

Do Not Dismiss a Change as "Just Old Age"

You know your dog's ordinary routines better than anyone. That makes your observations valuable—but you do not have to identify the diagnosis yourself.

If your old mate takes longer to rise, gives up favourite activities or no longer settles comfortably, record the change and talk with your veterinarian. Pain-assessment guidelines emphasise the importance of owner observations, especially with chronic conditions that may not be obvious during a short clinic visit.

The goal is not to make an ageing dog behave like a puppy again. It is to protect comfort, independence and the small daily pleasures that still matter: walking to the garden, greeting the family and resting without pain.
