Back Pain & Spondylosis in Older Dogs: Early Signs Every Aussie Owner Should Know

Spinal arthritis (spondylosis) is one of the most under-recognised causes of stiffness in ageing Aussie dogs. Learn the early warning signs, how vets diagnose it, and the practical management that keeps senior backs comfortable.

💡 Key Takeaway

Spondylosis — bony spurs growing along the spine — affects a large share of dogs over age 10, but many owners mistake it for "general slowing down." Watch for a stiff, hunched back, reluctance to turn or look up, and yelping when lifted. Caught early, it is very manageable; ignored, it can seriously limit mobility.

What Is Spondylosis & Spinal Arthritis?

Spondylosis deformans is a degenerative condition where the body grows bony spurs (osteophytes) along the edges of the vertebrae in an attempt to stabilise a stiffening spine. Over time these spurs can bridge neighbouring spinal bones. It's technically a form of spinal arthritis, and it's astonishingly common: studies suggest a meaningful proportion of dogs show some spondylosis by the time they reach their senior years, with rates climbing steeply after age 10.

Here's the confusing part — many dogs with spondylosis on X-ray show no obvious symptoms, while others with only mild changes are clearly sore. That's because the spurs only cause pain when they pinch nerves, restrict movement, or inflame the surrounding tissue. For an Australian owner, the practical takeaway is this: spondylosis is a "silent until it isn't" condition, which is exactly why knowing the early signs matters.

Early Warning Signs Most Owners Miss

Back pain in dogs rarely looks like a human clutching their lower back. It shows up as subtle changes in how your dog moves and behaves. The signs that should put "spinal arthritis" on your radar:

  • A stiff, hunched or "roached" back — the spine arches upward, especially after rest.
  • Reluctance to turn, circle, or look upward — your dog avoids twisting the spine.
  • Yelping or tensing when lifted — particularly being picked up under the chest or hindquarters.
  • Older dog stiffness that's worst first thing in the morning and eases after a short wander.
  • Difficulty with stairs, the car, or the couch — anything requiring spinal flexion.
  • Muscle loss along the spine or hindquarters from moving less.
  • Quieter, more withdrawn behaviour — some dogs simply "act older" without a clear limp.

Because these signs overlap with hip arthritis, it's worth noting one distinguishing clue: dogs with back pain often stand with their front legs braced and hind legs under them, almost "locked," whereas hip dogs tend to bunny-hop. When in doubt, your vet's hands and an X-ray settle it.

Spondylosis vs a Slipped Disc — Why It Matters

Not all back pain is spondylosis, and telling them apart can be the difference between "rest and supplements" and "emergency surgery." Two other big causes of spinal pain in older dogs are intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) — a slipped or bulging disc — and muscle strain. Here's the quick comparison:

🟡 Spondylosis
Gradual stiffness, hunched back, worse in cold. Often mild day-to-day.
Supplements, pain relief, physio
~$40–150/month
🔴 Slipped Disc (IVDD)
Sudden pain, crying, wobbly or dragging back legs, may lose bladder control.
Urgent — often surgery
$5,000–10,000+
🟢 Muscle Strain
Soreness after overexertion, eases with a day or two of rest.

$0–120

The red-flag version — sudden inability to walk, crying in pain, or loss of bowel/bladder control — is a true emergency. If you see it, don't wait for morning; contact an emergency vet. Spondylosis itself rarely causes those dramatic signs, which is reassuring, but it can coexist with a disc problem.

How Australian Vets Diagnose Back Pain

Diagnosis starts with a hands-on neurological and orthopaedic exam: your vet will assess your dog's gait, check spinal sensitivity, test reflexes, and feel for muscle wasting. They'll ask about the onset, what makes it better or worse, and any jumping or slipping incidents.

The definitive tool is imaging. X-rays reveal the bony spurs of spondylosis clearly and typically cost $200–$450. However, X-rays don't show the spinal cord or discs well, so if a slipped disc or nerve compression is suspected, your vet may recommend MRI or CT — usually at a referral hospital — which runs into the thousands. In many Australian cases, a confident spondylosis diagnosis from X-rays is enough to start management without the expense of advanced imaging.

Blood tests are sometimes run to rule out infection or other systemic causes, and your vet may suggest a brief trial of anti-inflammatory medication to confirm the pain is spinal in origin. If symptoms don't respond, further investigation follows.

Managing Spinal Arthritis at Home

The good news: most spondylosis is managed, not cured — and managed very successfully. The goal is to keep the spine mobile, the muscles supporting it strong, and the inflammation low.

Keep Movement Gentle and Regular

Short, frequent lead walks on flat ground keep the spine supple without overloading it. Avoid rough play, frisbee sprints, and steep hills. Swimming and hydrotherapy are excellent — warm water supports the body while building core strength. Many Australian clinics offer canine hydrotherapy; ask your vet for a referral.

Support the Spine at Home

Use a supportive harness (never tug on a collar attached to a sore neck), lay non-slip mats on polished floors, and consider a car ramp so your dog stops launching off the seat. Raised food bowls reduce neck strain. A memory-foam orthopaedic bed keeps the back aligned overnight.

Anti-Inflammatory and Pain Relief

Veterinary anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs made for dogs) are the mainstay for flare-ups, and monthly Librela injections are increasingly used by Australian vets for chronic joint and spinal pain. Never substitute human medication. Your vet tailors dose to your dog's weight, kidneys, and other conditions.

Supplements and Weight Control

Omega-3 fish oil, green-lipped mussel (a NZ staple), and joint formulas with glucosamine/MSM support spinal cartilage and quiet inflammation. Keeping your dog lean is critical — extra kilos load the whole axial skeleton. Even a few percent of body-weight loss noticeably eases back pain.

Physiotherapy and Heat

Certified canine physiotherapists use laser therapy, gentle massage, and targeted exercises. At home, a warm (not hot) wheat bag over the lower back for a few minutes can loosen morning stiffness — just never leave it unattended and always wrap it in a towel.

Building a Daily Routine Around Back Pain

Consistency beats intensity for a dog with spondylosis. They do best on a predictable, low-impact rhythm rather than weekend bursts of activity that inflame the spine. A pattern that works for many Australian households:

  • Morning: a few minutes of gentle lead walking to loosen the back, then breakfast from a raised bowl. Never force a stiff dog into a long walk before it has warmed up.
  • Midday: short puzzle-feeding or a sniff around the backyard — mental enrichment without spinal load. Keep jumps and rough play firmly off the menu.
  • Evening: a second short, flat walk, then a warm orthopaedic bed and, if prescribed, an evening pain-relief dose. A wrapped wheat bag takes the chill off the lower back before sleep.

A steady routine also makes problems visible: when your dog suddenly refuses the morning walk, that's an early flare signal rather than a lazy day. Many owners keep a simple fortnightly note of stiffness, appetite, and willingness to move — a habit your vet will thank you for at check-ups.

Winter Flare-Ups Across Australia

Cold, damp mornings reliably tighten arthritic spines, and Australian winters — especially across Victoria, Tasmania, and the southern states — bring more flare-ups. Keep bedding away from draughts, consider a heated pet pad on the coldest nights, and shift morning walks later once the sun has taken the edge off. If stiffness ramps up every winter, ask your vet about a seasonal top-up to the pain plan instead of waiting for a bad episode. Equally, humid summer storms can drop barometric pressure and stir old aches, so watch both ends of the weather year.
