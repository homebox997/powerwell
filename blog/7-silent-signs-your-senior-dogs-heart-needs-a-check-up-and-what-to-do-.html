7 Silent Signs Your Senior Dog’s Heart Needs a Check-Up (And What to Do Tonight)

Published July 26, 2026 · 4 min read

The Sneaky Signs Your Dog’s Heart Is Struggling

Your older mate might be slowing down, and you might chalk it up to ageing. But watch for these quiet hints: a dry, honking cough that gets worse when he lies down at night, tiring out on walks you used to sail through together, or breathing faster even when resting. A bloated belly that feels tight like a drum could mean fluid build-up, and a blue-tinged tongue or sudden collapse is a definite red flag.

Small breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Dachshunds, Chihuahuas and Pomeranians often develop leaky heart valves. Big dogs like Dobermans and Boxers are more prone to a weak heart muscle. If any of these signs sound familiar, don’t panic—but do pop into your vet.

Why That Annual Heart Scan Is a Game-Changer

Even if your vet can’t hear a murmur, a heart ultrasound (just like the ones used for pregnant bellies) can spot early valve changes. Vets now recommend small dogs over 7 years old get a yearly scan. It’s the best way to catch trouble before it truly kicks in.

Treating a dog before heart failure starts can delay that scary step by more than a year. Think of it like catching a tyre leak before it goes flat on the highway.

The Simple At-Home Test Every Owner Should Do

Counting your dog’s breaths while they’re sound asleep tells you more than guessing about coughs. Here’s how: set a timer for one minute, watch their chest rise and fall (don’t touch), and count.

  • Normal: under 30 breaths per minute.
  • Cause for a phone call: consistently over 35 breaths per minute, or breathing that looks harder than usual.

Jot the number in a notebook or your phone every few days to build a baseline. If it creeps up, you’ll spot it long before a crisis.

Tuck Away the Salty Treats (Yes, Even the Sausage Sanga)

Salt makes the body hold onto water, which adds extra strain to an already tired heart. That leftover snag from the barbie, a slice of cheese, deli ham, or even some jerky treats are loaded with sodium. We’ve seen dogs wind up in emergency with struggling lungs after a weekend of well-meaning snacks.

Stick to low-salt dog food, or whip up steamed beans, carrot sticks or pumpkin with no seasoning. And gently remind visitors and the grandkids: no sneaky treats under the table.

Medicine That Gives Dogs More Good Years

If your vet prescribes medication, it’s not a death sentence—it’s breathing room. A daily pill like pimobendan helps the heart pump stronger and widens blood vessels. Water pills (diuretics) clear fluid off the lungs. Others calm the blood pressure down.

In a big study, dogs with enlarged hearts who started pimobendan before any visible heart failure got an extra 15 months of happy life compared to those on a dummy pill. The key is giving every dose exactly as directed—never chop or change the dose yourself, and keep up regular vet visits for blood tests to check kidneys and salts.

A Pom’s Story: From Coughing All Night to Chasing Balls Again

Charlie, a 12-year-old desexed male Pomeranian, was up every night with a hacking cough and hardly wanted to play. His vet found a loud murmur and a chest X-ray showed fluid in his lungs. A heart ultrasound confirmed leaky valve disease. With a heart pill and a blood pressure med, the cough eased within weeks. Later his kidney numbers edged up, so the vet adjusted his water pill and switched him to a kidney-friendly diet. That was 1.5 years ago—Charlie still trots out to greet the postie every morning and snuggles through the night. He’s proof you can manage this at home.

Don’t Forget the Pearly Whites

Gunky teeth can send bacteria into the blood and infect dodgy heart valves, a condition that’s rare but serious. If your dog already has valve trouble, your vet may give antibiotics before a dental clean. Best defence: daily brushing with dog-friendly toothpaste, plus a professional scale and polish when your vet advises.

Omega-3s: A Little Oil Goes a Long Way

Fish oil rich in EPA and DHA fights inflammation and helps keep muscle on ageing frames. A typical dose for a 10kg dog is around 400–800 mg of combined EPA+DHA a day (ask your vet for your dog’s perfect measure). Pick a high-purity fish oil without mountains of added vitamins A and D, and always check with your vet if your dog is on other meds, especially heart rhythm pills.

Your grey-muzzled best mate is part of the family. A few simple checks at home, a smart diet, and a team-up with your vet can mean more years of beach strolls, park sniffs, and couch cuddles. If something feels off, trust your gut and give your vet a call—you know your dog better than anyone.
