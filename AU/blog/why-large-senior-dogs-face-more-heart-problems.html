Why Large Senior Dogs Face More Heart Problems

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 3 August 2026 · Status: SOURCE CHECKED — VET REVIEW REQUIRED

Quick answer: Large dogs are not destined to develop heart disease, and size alone is not a diagnosis. However, several large and giant breeds are more prone to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a disease that weakens the heart muscle. Age can reveal changes that were previously silent, while excess body fat and other health problems may add strain or make early signs easier to miss.

A Labrador who pauses halfway up the garden path, a Dobermann who no longer wants the second lap of the oval, or a Great Dane who takes longer to settle after a warm Melbourne walk may simply be tired. They may also be showing an early health change. The useful question is not “Is this just because my dog is big?” but “Is this new for my dog, and is it happening more often?”

It is not simply a bigger heart working harder

Large dogs naturally have larger hearts to serve larger bodies. That fact by itself does not mean their hearts are failing. Heart risk depends on the particular disease involved. Small dogs, for example, commonly develop degenerative valve disease, while large and giant dogs receive special attention for DCM.

DCM affects the heart muscle. The pumping chambers can enlarge and the muscle can lose some of its ability to contract. The condition is especially recognised in breeds including Dobermann Pinschers, Great Danes, Irish Wolfhounds and some other large dogs. Genetics are important in certain breeds, although illness, toxins, nutritional factors and unknown causes can also be involved. A mixed-breed large dog can develop heart disease too, and a high-risk breed may never develop it.

How breed, age and body condition fit together

Breed changes the type of risk

A breed tendency is a reason to be observant, not a verdict. If your dog belongs to a breed with a recognised DCM risk, ask your vet whether routine listening is enough or whether screening should be discussed before symptoms appear. The answer will depend on age, family history, examination findings and the breed.

Age makes regular comparison more valuable

DCM is often identified in middle-aged or older dogs. Ageing also brings other changes—arthritis, loss of muscle, weight gain and reduced fitness—that can look like heart trouble or hide it. This is why a trend matters more than a single slow day. A senior check gives your vet a chance to compare heart sounds, pulse, breathing, body condition and activity with earlier visits.

Body fat can blur the picture

Being overweight should not be presented as a simple cause of DCM. It can, however, make walking harder, increase heat intolerance and add demand during activity. A dog may appear “unfit” when several issues are overlapping. Ask your vet to assess both body condition and muscle condition; a heavy coat can make changes difficult to see at home.

Changes worth noticing at home

Heart disease can be quiet early on. None of the signs below proves that the heart is the cause, because lung disease, pain, anaemia, heat and poor fitness can produce similar changes. Record what you see and let your vet sort out the possibilities.

Get urgent veterinary help now if your dog is struggling to breathe, collapses, cannot stand, or has blue, grey or very pale gums. Keep them calm, minimise movement and call the nearest emergency veterinary service while arranging transport.

A simple heart-health note for your phone

Once a week, jot down how your dog handles one familiar activity. Use the same short route or household routine rather than deliberately testing their limits. Note coughing, unusual panting, appetite, sleep, body weight if available, and any episodes of weakness. A 10-second video of a new cough or change in movement can be more useful to your vet than trying to remember it later.

Do not start a demanding exercise plan to “strengthen the heart,” and do not sharply restrict normal activity without veterinary guidance. Choose cooler parts of the day, carry water and let your senior dog set a comfortable pace. In Australia, parasite prevention also needs to match local risk, travel and veterinary advice; heartworm disease is different from DCM but can affect the heart and lungs.

What your vet may consider

A routine visit may begin with history, a physical examination and listening to the heart and lungs. Depending on what is found, a vet may discuss an electrocardiogram to assess rhythm, chest X-rays, heart ultrasound or blood tests. Not every older large dog needs every test. The purpose is to choose the right investigation for the individual dog, not to run a standard package because of body size.

Keep this in mind: Large size matters mainly because some large breeds are predisposed to particular heart-muscle diseases. Age, genetics, body condition and other illnesses shape the full picture. Watching for change—and sharing clear notes with your vet—is more useful than assuming that slowing down is inevitable.

Frequently asked questions

Do all large dogs develop heart disease?

No. Large body size does not guarantee heart disease. Risk varies with the type of heart condition, breed, age, family history and the individual dog’s health.

Which heart problem is especially important in large breeds?

Dilated cardiomyopathy, or DCM, is particularly important in several large and giant breeds. It weakens the heart muscle and reduces pumping ability, but only a veterinarian can diagnose it.

Can an overweight dog develop DCM because of its weight?

Excess weight is not the same thing as DCM and should not be described as its direct cause. It can add physical demand, reduce exercise tolerance and make breathing or mobility changes harder to interpret.

Should a healthy-looking senior dog have heart checks?

Yes. Routine senior examinations allow a vet to listen to the heart and lungs, compare changes over time and decide whether further testing is appropriate for that dog.
