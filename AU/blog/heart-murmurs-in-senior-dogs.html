Senior Dog Heart Health

Heart Murmurs in Senior Dogs: What They Mean and When to Act

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 10 August 2026

Quick answer: A heart murmur is an extra sound a veterinarian hears when blood flow becomes turbulent. It is a clue, not a diagnosis, and it does not by itself mean that your senior dog is in heart failure. The next step depends on the murmur’s timing and location, your dog’s breed and symptoms, and whether tests show a structural or rhythm problem.

The appointment may have felt completely routine. Your dog walked in for a senior check, accepted a biscuit and looked ready to leave. Then the veterinarian listened a little longer than usual and said, “I can hear a heart murmur.” It is a small sentence that can make the drive home feel much longer.

Try not to turn the word murmur into a prognosis. Some dogs have a murmur for years without developing heart failure. Others need investigation sooner because of the type of murmur, an abnormal rhythm, a recognised breed risk or changes already happening at home. What matters now is turning one listening finding into a clear, sensible plan.

Look at the dog, not only the murmur: Seek emergency veterinary care if your dog is struggling to breathe at rest, cannot lie down comfortably, has blue or grey gums, collapses or faints, becomes profoundly weak, or deteriorates quickly. Keep activity and stress low while arranging care.

What the veterinarian is actually hearing

A normal heartbeat has distinct sounds created as heart valves close. A murmur is an added whooshing or swishing sound caused by turbulent blood flow. The sound may be produced when a valve leaks, when blood moves through a narrowed opening, when blood flow is unusually fast, or when an abnormal connection changes the route blood takes.

The murmur does not reveal the entire condition through a stethoscope. The veterinarian describes where it is loudest, when it occurs within the heartbeat, how widely it can be heard and how intense it sounds. Those details narrow the possibilities, but imaging or other tests may be needed to see what is happening inside the heart.

A murmur is also different from an arrhythmia, which means an abnormal heart rhythm. A dog can have a murmur, an arrhythmia, both or neither. Listening may raise concern about rhythm, but an electrocardiogram records the heart’s electrical activity and answers a different question.

Why a murmur may appear during the senior years

Some heart diseases become more common or more noticeable with age. Myxomatous mitral valve disease, often shortened to MMVD, causes a heart valve to thicken and leak. It is especially common in older small-breed dogs, although larger dogs can be affected. A murmur may be heard months or even years before signs of congestive heart failure appear.

Large and giant senior dogs bring a slightly different set of questions. Dilated cardiomyopathy, or DCM, affects the heart muscle and is recognised in breeds including Dobermann Pinschers, Great Danes and Irish Wolfhounds. A dog with DCM may have a soft murmur, an abnormal rhythm or no obvious murmur. This is why a reassuringly quiet chest sound cannot replace breed-appropriate screening or investigation of fainting, weakness and reduced stamina.

A newly heard murmur does not always mean the disease began recently. The sound may have been too soft to hear before, the consulting room may previously have been noisy, or the dog’s excitement and heart rate may change how easy it is to detect. Different body shapes and breathing sounds also affect listening. Comparing notes across visits is helpful.

Occasionally, increased blood flow associated with conditions outside the heart—such as anaemia—can contribute to a murmur. That possibility is one reason a broad senior examination and basic laboratory work may matter. It is not safe to assume that every murmur comes from the same valve problem.

What a murmur grade tells you—and what it does not

Veterinarians commonly grade murmurs on a scale from I to VI. Grade I is very soft; grade VI is very loud and can often be felt as vibration through the chest wall. The grade provides a consistent way to describe and follow the sound.

It is tempting to treat the number as a heart-failure score. It is not. In some diseases, intensity relates reasonably well to severity. In others, a soft murmur may accompany important disease, while a loud murmur may occur in a dog who is comfortable and has never had heart failure. The grade must be interpreted alongside the murmur’s location and timing, the dog’s pulse and rhythm, clinical signs, and any imaging.

One useful question at the appointment: “What does this grade mean for the particular type of murmur you heard?” That invites a more helpful answer than simply asking whether the number is good or bad.

How quickly should you act?

The safest timing depends more on your dog’s condition than on the existence of the murmur alone.

What the next examination may include

A careful history

The veterinarian may ask about coughs, breathing, familiar walks, fainting episodes, appetite, weight and sleep. They may also ask about previous illnesses, current medicines, supplements and family or breed history. A dog who seems normal in a consulting room can still have a meaningful pattern at home, so your observations matter.

Listening beyond the murmur

The vet listens to heart rate and rhythm, lungs and the location of the sound. They may feel pulses while listening to check whether every heartbeat produces an effective pulse. Gum colour, breathing effort, abdominal shape, body condition and muscle condition add context.

Checking the wider senior-health picture

Blood and urine tests do not show the heart’s structure, but they can identify anaemia and assess organs that influence investigation and treatment choices. Blood pressure can also be relevant. A dog may have heart disease and kidney disease, arthritis or another condition at the same time; a plan needs to fit the whole dog.

Which heart test answers which question?

Echocardiography is ultrasound of the heart. It shows valves, chamber size, wall movement and blood flow. It is often the clearest way to identify the structural cause of a murmur and measure whether the heart has enlarged. The person performing it needs appropriate training and the result still has to be interpreted with the dog’s history.

Chest X-rays show the heart’s outline and, importantly, the lungs and airways. They may help a veterinarian look for fluid in the lungs or another reason for coughing and breathing changes. X-rays and echocardiography are complementary rather than interchangeable.

Electrocardiography, or an ECG, records electrical rhythm. A brief ECG may detect an abnormality present at that moment. If weakness or fainting is intermittent, longer ambulatory monitoring—often called a Holter—may be discussed, particularly in dogs at risk of intermittent rhythm problems.

Cardiac biomarkers are blood tests that may add information about heart-muscle stretch or injury in selected cases. They are not a stand-alone answer to “Does this murmur matter?” and do not replace imaging when the structure of the heart needs to be assessed.

The right starting point varies. An older small dog with a typical left-sided murmur and no signs may follow one pathway. A Dobermann with fainting, a Boxer with an irregular rhythm or a Labrador with coughing may need a different sequence because the questions are different.

What to watch at home without turning life into a medical test

You do not need to listen to the chest or calculate a pulse. Inaccurate measurements create worry, and repeated handling may make your dog restless. Instead, collect a few observations that reflect everyday function.

  1. Learn the sleeping breathing pattern. Count only while your dog is genuinely asleep, not panting, dreaming or just back from activity. Record the number and any visible effort. Ask your vet what trend should prompt a call for this dog.
  2. Compare the same gentle walk. Note willingness to start, number of stops and recovery afterwards. Keep weather and route in mind; a hot Melbourne afternoon is not comparable with a cool morning.
  3. Describe coughs precisely. Record time, trigger, duration and recovery. A safe short video is often more useful than an imitation of the sound.
  4. Notice sleep and settling. Repeatedly changing position, sitting upright to sleep or seeming unable to get comfortable can be important, especially with breathing change.
  5. Record fainting or sudden weakness. Note what happened immediately before and how long recovery took, but do not delay urgent care to finish a video or diary entry.

Keep the record light enough that you can maintain it. A short note three times a week often reveals more than a detailed chart abandoned after two days. The aim is to show a trend, not to prove a diagnosis.

Three common misunderstandings after a murmur is found

“No symptoms means there is no disease”

Some dogs have structural heart disease before clinical signs appear. In the ACVIM system for mitral valve disease, these preclinical dogs are separated according to whether there is enough heart enlargement to meet specific criteria. That distinction requires assessment; an owner cannot determine it from the murmur grade.

“Every murmur needs heart medicine immediately”

Treatment is based on the cause and stage, not the sound alone. Some dogs need monitoring rather than medication. Others benefit from treatment once defined findings are present. Starting a borrowed tablet, changing a dose or giving an extra diuretic can affect blood pressure, hydration, electrolytes and kidney function. Use only the plan given for your dog.

“A louder murmur means heart failure has begun”

Heart failure is a clinical condition in which the heart cannot meet the body’s needs without harmful congestion or other consequences. Diagnosis may involve signs, examination and imaging. A louder sound can be worth reassessing, but volume alone does not diagnose fluid in the lungs.

Questions that turn uncertainty into a plan

  • Where is the murmur loudest, when in the heartbeat does it occur, and what causes are you considering?
  • Was the rhythm regular, and were the pulses normal?
  • Does my dog’s breed or family history change the recommended testing?
  • Would an echocardiogram, chest X-ray or ECG change what we do now?
  • What should I record at home, and how often?
  • Which signs mean I should call today, and which mean I should go straight to emergency?
  • When should the murmur and the overall heart picture be rechecked?

Write the answers down. “Recheck later” is much more useful when later has a date and the family knows which changes should shorten the wait.

Keep this in mind: A murmur is the beginning of a question, not the end of the story. Your dog may need monitoring, further testing or treatment, but the decision should be based on the likely cause, stage and whole health picture. Watch breathing, comfort and function; keep the follow-up plan; and act quickly if serious symptoms appear.

Frequently asked questions

Does a heart murmur mean my senior dog has heart failure?

No. A murmur is a sound made by turbulent blood flow. It can be associated with structural heart disease, another health condition or, less commonly in an older dog, a temporary change in blood flow. Heart failure requires evidence beyond the murmur itself.

Can a quiet murmur still matter?

Yes. Loudness helps describe a murmur, but it does not reliably measure the seriousness of every condition. Timing, location, symptoms and appropriate testing all matter.

Does every dog with a murmur need an echocardiogram?

Not automatically. The veterinarian uses age, breed, symptoms, murmur features and the rest of the examination to decide whether monitoring, imaging or rhythm testing is appropriate.

Should a dog start heart medicine as soon as a murmur is found?

Not simply because a murmur is present. Treatment depends on the cause and stage. Do not start, stop or borrow heart medicine without a veterinarian’s plan.

When is a heart murmur an emergency?

The murmur itself is not usually the emergency. Seek immediate care for difficult breathing at rest, blue or grey gums, collapse or fainting, marked weakness, inability to settle or rapid deterioration.
