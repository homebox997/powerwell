Senior Dog Breathing Fast or Heavy: When to Worry

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 4 August 2026

Go now, not after another count: If your dog is visibly struggling for air, breathing with an open mouth while resting, stretching the neck to breathe, cannot lie down comfortably, has blue, grey or very pale gums, collapses, or is getting worse quickly, contact an emergency vet immediately. Keep your dog cool and quiet, minimise walking, and phone the clinic while arranging transport.
Quick answer: Faster breathing in a large senior dog matters most when it happens during genuine rest or sleep, repeats, rises above that dog's usual pattern, or comes with extra effort. Heart failure can cause a faster sleeping respiratory rate, but it is not the only explanation. Pain, heat, anxiety, fever, anaemia, airway or lung disease, and several other problems can look similar. Count calmly, look at effort as well as speed, and let a vet investigate a persistent change.

It is often the quietest moment that makes an owner pause. Your dog is asleep after dinner, the house is still, yet the chest seems to be moving more quickly than usual. Or perhaps the breathing sounds heavier, the belly is joining every breath, and your dog keeps lifting the head instead of settling.

That is different from panting after a warm Melbourne afternoon or an excited greeting at the door. The useful question is not simply, "Is my dog breathing fast?" It is, "What is happening while my dog is truly resting, and is this a change from normal?"

Breathing rate and breathing effort are not the same thing

Breathing rate is the number of breaths taken in one minute. Breathing effort describes how hard the body appears to work for each breath. A dog can breathe a little faster without obvious struggle, or breathe at a less dramatic rate while using the belly, neck and chest muscles in an abnormal way.

Effort can be the more urgent clue. Watch from a short distance before touching or moving your dog. Concerning changes include the abdomen pushing strongly with each breath, nostrils flaring, elbows held away from the chest, the head and neck stretched forward, repeated changes of position, or an inability to lie flat and relax.

Noise matters too, but sound alone cannot tell you where the problem is. Wheezing, harsh breathing, snoring that is new, grunting, or a wet-sounding cough may point toward different parts of the airway, lungs or circulation. A video can be very helpful to a vet, provided filming does not delay care.

How to count a sleeping respiratory rate

A sleeping respiratory rate is usually more useful than a count taken while your dog is alert, panting, worried or watching you. Wait until your dog is fully asleep in a comfortable position. Avoid counting during dream movements, twitching, active panting or immediately after exercise.

  1. Watch the chest or flank without waking your dog.
  2. Count one complete rise and fall as one breath.
  3. Count for 30 seconds and multiply by two, or count for a full minute.
  4. Write down the result, time, room temperature and anything unusual that day.
  5. Repeat on several calm occasions so you learn your dog's own baseline.

Veterinary references commonly describe a sleeping respiratory rate below about 30 breaths per minute as normal for many dogs. That number is a useful guide, not a home diagnosis. Individual instructions may differ, particularly if your dog has known heart disease and your veterinarian has set a personal action threshold.

Why heart disease can change breathing

The heart's job is to move blood through the lungs and around the body. In left-sided congestive heart failure, pressure can build behind the left side of the heart and fluid may collect in lung tissue. This is called pulmonary oedema. Affected dogs may breathe faster, work harder to breathe, cough, tire easily or struggle to settle.

Large and giant breeds also receive attention because some are predisposed to diseases of the heart muscle, including dilated cardiomyopathy. But breed and age do not make every breathing change cardiac. A murmur, an enlarged heart on an earlier image, or a known diagnosis changes the level of concern, yet the current cause still needs clinical assessment.

Other reasons a senior dog may breathe faster or harder

Rapid breathing is a sign, not a diagnosis. Some causes are mild and temporary; others are emergencies. This is why context matters.

Possible influence Clues you may notice What the clue cannot prove
Heat or recent activity Panting begins after exercise or in a warm room and eases as the dog cools and rests Improvement does not rule out underlying heart or airway disease
Pain Restlessness, trembling, guarding, difficulty rising, a tense abdomen or reluctance to move Dogs may hide pain, and the painful area may not be obvious
Anxiety or excitement Wide eyes, pacing, attention to a trigger, improvement when calm Do not assume anxiety when breathing effort is present
Fever or systemic illness Lethargy, reduced appetite, warmth, vomiting or other illness signs A warm nose cannot diagnose fever
Airway or lung disease Coughing, noisy breathing, nasal discharge, altered bark, exercise intolerance Sound and cough type cannot identify the cause at home
Heart or circulatory disease Rising sleeping rate, cough, weakness, fainting, abdominal swelling or reduced stamina No single sign confirms heart failure

A calm home check that takes two minutes

If your dog is comfortable and no emergency sign is present, observe before intervening. Ask:

  • Is my dog asleep, quietly resting, panting, or recently active?
  • What is the one-minute rate, and how does it compare with previous sleeping counts?
  • Is the breathing shallow, unusually deep, noisy or irregular?
  • Is the abdomen working harder than normal?
  • Can my dog lie down and remain settled?
  • Are the gums their normal pink colour?
  • Is there coughing, weakness, fainting, poor appetite, fever, pain or a swollen abdomen?

Do not repeatedly open the mouth, press on the chest, force your dog to walk, or hold a distressed dog tightly. Stress and handling can increase oxygen demand. If you are uncertain whether the effort is abnormal, phone a veterinary clinic and describe exactly what you see.

What to do tonight if the dog is comfortable but the rate is higher

Move your dog to a quiet, comfortably cool area and allow full rest. Recount only after the dog is genuinely asleep. Record two or three readings rather than staring continuously for hours. Note any medication already given at the normal prescribed time, but do not change doses unless your veterinarian has provided a written action plan for this exact situation.

Contact your regular vet promptly if the sleeping rate remains above your dog's baseline, is repeatedly above the threshold your vet gave you, or has been trending upward over several days. A same-day assessment may be appropriate when the change is accompanied by coughing, reduced appetite, unusual fatigue or difficulty becoming comfortable.

If effort develops, colour changes, your dog cannot settle, or the condition worsens, stop monitoring and seek emergency care. The goal of home counting is earlier recognition—not delaying a needed examination while waiting for a "perfect" number.

Keep this in mind: A sleeping respiratory rate is valuable because it turns a vague worry into a trend your vet can use. It is not a pass-or-fail heart test. Watch the whole dog, treat visible effort as more urgent than arithmetic, and act on a real change from your dog's comfortable normal.

Frequently asked questions

How do I count my dog's sleeping breathing rate?

Wait until your dog is fully asleep and not dreaming or panting. Count one chest rise and fall as one breath for 30 seconds, then multiply by two. Write down the result and repeat on calm nights so you know the usual range.

Does fast breathing mean my dog has heart failure?

No. Heart failure is one possible cause, but pain, fever, heat, anxiety, anaemia, airway disease and lung disease can also increase breathing. A repeated change at rest or any increase in effort needs veterinary advice.

When is breathing an emergency?

Go urgently for obvious effort at rest, open-mouth breathing unrelated to heat or exercise, blue or grey gums, collapse, inability to settle or lie down, or rapid worsening. Minimise movement and call the emergency clinic during transport arrangements.
