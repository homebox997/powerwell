What Is a Heart Murmur and Should You Worry?

If your vet has told you that your dog has a heart murmur, you might feel a bit panicked. But here's the good news: a murmur doesn't automatically mean heart failure. Think of it like a squeaky door hinge – it's just a sign that blood is flowing a little noisily through the heart. For many dogs, especially small breeds like Cavaliers and Dachshunds, a murmur can be present for years without causing any trouble.

Meet Myxomatous Mitral Valve Disease (MMVD)

MMVD is the most common heart disease in dogs, responsible for about 75% of all canine heart issues. It's especially common in small breeds – nearly every Cavalier King Charles Spaniel will develop it by age 10. Other breeds at risk include Miniature Poodles, Cocker Spaniels, and Dachshunds. The condition involves a gradual thickening of the heart valve, which then leaks a little. Over time, the heart works harder to compensate, and eventually it may struggle to keep up.

Spotting the Signs Early

One of the tricky things about MMVD is that it can be silent for years. Your dog might seem perfectly healthy, playing fetch and enjoying walks, all while having a heart murmur. That's why regular vet checkups are so important. As the disease progresses, you might notice:

  • Coughing, especially at night or after exercise
  • Rapid or laboured breathing
  • Getting tired more quickly on walks
  • Restlessness or trouble settling
  • Fainting or collapsing (in severe cases)
  • Swollen belly (fluid buildup)

Chip, a 10-year-old Cavalier, started coughing at night and seemed tired on walks. His owner took him for a checkup, and the vet found a murmur. An echocardiogram confirmed MMVD with early heart failure. Thanks to quick treatment, Chip's coughing reduced and he was back to his happy self within weeks.

Busting the Myth: A Murmur ≠ Heart Failure

One of the biggest misconceptions is that a heart murmur means your dog is about to go into heart failure. In reality, many dogs live for years with a murmur and never develop symptoms. Heart failure happens only when the heart can no longer pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. If your dog has a murmur but no other signs, your vet will likely recommend monitoring and regular checkups rather than immediate treatment.

When Treatment Is Needed: Pimobendan and ACE Inhibitors

If your dog does develop congestive heart failure (CHF), the good news is that there are effective treatments. The first-line medication is pimobendan, which strengthens the heart's contractions and widens blood vessels. It's given twice daily on an empty stomach. Studies show it can nearly double survival time – from about 140 days to 267 days in one trial.

ACE inhibitors like enalapril are also used to reduce fluid retention and lower blood pressure. But they're not for every dog – they're best for those with heart enlargement or CHF. Starting them too early, before symptoms appear, isn't recommended.

Managing Diuretics Safely

Diuretics like furosemide help remove excess fluid from the lungs and belly. But they must be used carefully. Never stop or reduce the dose without your vet's guidance, as this can cause a dangerous fluid buildup. On the flip side, too much can lead to dehydration and kidney issues. Always follow your vet's instructions to the letter.

Lifestyle Tips to Keep Your Dog Comfortable

While MMVD is progressive, there's plenty you can do to help your furry friend feel their best:

  • Keep them lean – extra weight puts more strain on the heart.
  • Moderate exercise – short, gentle walks are great; avoid strenuous activity.
  • Low-sodium diet – ask your vet for recommendations.
  • Reduce stress – keep a calm environment and avoid sudden excitement.
  • Regular vet checkups – early detection is key.

When to See Your Vet

If your dog has a heart murmur, schedule regular checkups (every 6–12 months) and watch for any changes in behaviour. If you notice coughing, breathing changes, or lethargy, don't wait – book an appointment. Early treatment can make a huge difference in your dog's quality of life.

The Bottom Line

A heart murmur isn't a death sentence. With regular monitoring, the right medications, and a few lifestyle tweaks, many dogs with MMVD live happy, active lives for years. Your vet is your best partner in managing this condition, so keep them in the loop and don't hesitate to ask questions. Your furry mate deserves the best care – and you've got this.
