A 9-Year-Old Cocker Spaniel's Sudden Pot Belly: What Every Aussie Dog Owner Should Know About Rare Heart Tumours

Published July 26, 2026 · 5 min read

A Real-Life Wake-Up Call from the Park

Imagine you're at the local off-leash area and a mate mentions their older dog has been looking a bit “off” lately—breathing a bit harder, tummy looking rounder. A few days later, that same dog collapses and the vet finds fluid everywhere. It sounds extreme, but it’s exactly what happened to an otherwise happy 9-year-old Cocker Spaniel here in Australia. Her story could help save your own dog’s life one day.

The sweet girl, an unspayed cocker spaniel, was rushed in because she suddenly couldn’t catch her breath and her belly had blown up like a balloon over just a few days. When the vet ultrasounded her chest and belly, they found fluid building up in three places: around her lungs, in her belly, and inside the sac that surrounds the heart. Even more worrying, they saw a large mass filling almost the entire right atrium—the chamber that receives blood coming back from the body. That blockage was stopping blood from returning normally, causing the classic signs of right-side heart failure.

After she was gently put to rest because the damage was too severe, a post-mortem (an animal version of an autopsy) revealed the culprit: a primary heart lymphoma, a cancer that starts in the heart muscle itself. It wasn’t the more common blood-vessel tumour (haemangiosarcoma) that many of us have heard about. This 40x35mm lump had been quietly growing until it nearly blocked the whole chamber.

What Right-Side Heart Failure Actually Looks Like

When the right side of the heart can’t pump properly, blood backs up through the body. In dogs, this often shows up as two very obvious changes:

  • Breathing trouble that comes on fast. You might see your dog panting heavily even when resting, or breathing with more effort than usual.
  • A swollen belly that’s not just weight gain. Called ascites, this is fluid leaking into the tummy because of high pressure in the veins. The belly can look tight and feel tense, like an overfilled beach ball.

Some dogs may also have less energy, a blueish tinge to their gums, or even collapse. If your dog—especially a senior—develops one or both of these signs within a day or two, it’s a genuine emergency. Don’t wait until morning; call your closest vet clinic right away.

Why This Rare Case Matters for Your Dog

The most common heart tumour in dogs is indeed haemangiosarcoma, but this case reminds us that lymphomas can also start inside the heart. Because lymphoma often responds to chemotherapy, missing it means missing a chance to give a dog extra good months—if it’s caught before severe heart failure sets in.

The vet team stressed that whenever an ultrasound shows a mass in the right atrium, they should consider lymphoma, not just the usual suspects. Gathering a tiny sample of fluid or tissue (which is often safer than it sounds) can make all the difference. The fluid from the belly in this case also gave a clue: it was a modified transudate, meaning it had slightly higher protein and cell counts, often seen when veins have been under pressure for a while—a nudge to look at the heart rather than just the liver.

Not All Heart Tumours Are What You Think

It’s easy, even for vets, to assume a heart mass in a dog equals haemangiosarcoma and that nothing can be done. The truth is, other cancers like lymphoma, mesothelioma or fibrosarcoma can pop up there too. A cocker spaniel with primary heart lymphoma might have been a candidate for chemo if it had been found earlier. So, if your dog ever needs a heart scan and a mass is spotted, ask your vet gently whether gathering more information could be worth it—especially if your dog is still bright and eating well.

What You Can Do Tonight: Early Heart Checks for Older Dogs

This story isn’t meant to scare you, but to arm you with simple habits. Heart tumours rarely show any symptoms until they’re large. By the time you see a swollen belly or laboured breathing, the window is tiny. Here’s what you can do:

  • Yearly vet checks for dogs over 7. Ask about a heart ultrasound during your senior pooch’s annual visit. It’s non-invasive and takes just a few minutes. Many clinics in Australia offer wellness packages that include ultrasound screening.
  • Know the high-risk breeds. Cocker Spaniels, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds, and other larger breeds seem to pop up more often in heart tumour cases. If you’ve got one of these beauties, a little extra vigilance goes a long way.
  • Don’t ignore a subtle pot belly. If your dog’s tummy looks rounder and you can’t pin it on extra snacks, have it checked. The same goes for any unexplained increase in panting or restlessness at night.
  • When fluid is found, ask about sampling. If your dog ever needs fluid drained due to heart failure, ask the vet if analysing that fluid can give clues about cancer type. It’s not always possible, but it’s a conversation worth having.

No one can prevent all cancers, but catching a heart mass when it’s the size of a pea rather than a golf ball means more options. That could be the difference between a peaceful goodbye and more sunny arvo walks along the coast.

This cocker spaniel’s story is a bittersweet reminder to love our seniors a little tighter and to listen when their bodies whisper. If you ever spot the signs, you’ll know what to do.
