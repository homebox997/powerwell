Is Your Senior Large-Breed Dog Hiding Pain? Subtle Signs to Check

August 06, 2026 · 6 min read · PawWell Editorial

Many Aussie dog mums and dads believe that if their old boy isn't yelping or crying, he can't be in real pain. It's a myth that can quietly steal a dog's comfort. Our big breedsthose Labrador, Golden Retriever, Rottweiler, and Mastiff seniorsare masters at hiding discomfort. A tail wag at the back door doesn't mean their joints don't ache. This guide helps you read the quiet signals your dog is sending, so you can step in early and keep their golden years truly comfortable.

Why a dog who doesn't cry can still be hurting

Pain isn't always loud. In the wild, showing weakness put a pack animal at risk, so dogs evolved to hide their hurt. That instinct lives on in your gentle giant. A nine-year-old Bernese Mountain Dog might be managing significant hip pain while still nudging your hand for a pat. The absence of a whimper doesn't mean the absence of painit means the dog is doing what his ancestors did: getting on with it.

Instead of obvious cries, look for tiny changes that slowly pile up. The morning stiffness that wears off. The back-step hesitation. The fetch toy that's carried for one lap instead of five. These are your large breed's way of coping, not a sign that everything is fine.

An everyday Aussie scene that tells a story

Picture this: you're sipping a cuppa on the back deck. Your old Labrador, Max, wobbles to his feet after a nap, takes a few stiff steps, then trots fairly well across the lawn. By the time you reach for the lead, he's wagging hard. It's easy to label that as 'just old age'. But inside those joints, the 'quiet' pain of arthritis could be doing its daily work.

Illustrative scenario not a real patient. Indy, a ten-year-old Golden Retriever from Sydney's northern beaches, started avoiding the longer coastal walk his owner loved. He'd still go to the park, but he'd lie down after ten minutes of fetch. His owner assumed he was simply slowing down. A vet check found moderate elbow arthritis. With a tailored pain plan, Indy returned to a shorter but happier beach strolland his family learned to 'listen' to what Indy wasn't saying.

The body language of hidden painwhat to actually watch for

You don't need a vet degree to spot changes; you just need a baseline. Large breed seniors often show pain through behaviour, not noise. Keep an eye on these day-to-day clues:

  • Rising slowly or 'rocking' to stand. A dog who used to spring up from his bed now pushes up with his front legs, or takes a few steps before his back end seems to catch up.
  • The limp that appears after rest. A subtle hitch in the first few steps that fades within a minute. Many owners call it 'warming out of it'but that's often a marker of joint pain.
  • Jumping or stair reluctance. Your German Shepherd who once leaped into the ute now pauses, or tries a 'sticky-beak' at the couch instead of jumping up.
  • More licking than usual. A dog licking a wrist, hock, or shoulder repeatedly can be trying to soothe an achy spot. If the area looks pinker than normal hairless patches, note it.
  • Shifting sleep patterns. Restlessness at night, frequent repositioning, or a dog who suddenly sleeps away from the family may be coping with discomfort.
  • Small changes in appetite or mood. A normally food-driven Labrador leaving a few biscuits in the bowl, or a Rottweiler who's less keen on ear rubsthese can be red herrings for pain.

None of these signs are dramatic on their own. Put two or three together, though, and you've got a dog who would likely feel a whole lot better with some vet-guided support.

Tonight's at-home self-check checklist

You can do this quiet, no-pressure check right nowno equipment needed. It's not a diagnosis, just a way to collect observations for your next vet chat.

  • Watch the rise. Call your dog from his bed or favourite spot. Is there a noticeable stiff-legged stretch before moving? Multiple attempts to stand? A soft groan? Record what you see.
  • Walk a straight line on a firm surface. Lead your dog across the kitchen or driveway. Look for a head bob (which often means the opposite leg is sore), a shortened stride on one side, or a lowered tail carriage.
  • Test the 'sit-to-stand'. Ask for a sit, then a walk. A painful dog may 'plop' the rear down, or avoid sitting squarely.
  • Observe stair behaviour. Even a single step indoors. Does he take it one leg at a time? Hesitate? Avoid jumping down?
  • Check for touch sensitivity. Gently run your hand down the spine, over the hips, and around the elbows. If your dog flinches, pulls away, or gives you a tight-mouthed look, that area might be tender.
  • Note night-time restlessness. This evening, if your dog paces or changes beds repeatedly, jot it down. A dog who can't settle might be hurting.

Keep a simple diary for a few days. When you bring that information to your veterinarian, it paints a clearer picture than a single clinic visit ever could. And before you add any joint supplement or change diets, have a brief chat with your vetsome products work well for large breeds, but the dose matters.

When to call your vet right away

Sometimes the signs aren't subtle, and that's your dog asking for urgent help. Trust your gut if something shifts suddenly.

If your dog cannot put any weight on a leg, has obvious swelling or deformity after an injury, shows sudden severe pain (vocalising, flinching, hiding), or seems unable to stand at all this needs urgent vet assessment. Do not give human pain medication.

Human painkillers like ibuprofen or paracetamol can be fatally toxic to dogs, even in small amounts. Never share your own medicine. If you're worried about pain overnight, contact your local emergency vet for guidance.

Simple steps that make a big difference

Living with a stoic senior doesn't mean you're helpless. Little adjustments at home can take the pressure off aching joints while you work with your vet on a long-term plan.

  • Non-slip surfaces are a gift. Run a cheap yoga mat or rubber-backed runner across polished boards or tiles. Slipping is painfuland scaryfor an unsteady big dog.
  • Ramps and raised feeders. A folding ramp for the ute or back steps saves jarring impact. Raised bowls let a sore-necked dog eat without straining.
  • Shorter, gentler exercise. Swap the hour-long beach run for a quiet sniffy walk on soft grass. Swimming is gold for large breeds if your vet gives the nodit builds muscle without pounding joints.
  • Warmth and weight management. A soft, well-cushioned bed (think memory foam) keeps pressure points happy. And keeping your dog lean is the single biggest kindness you can offer painful joints; even a couple of extra kilos on a Labrador's frame multiplies the load on arthritic elbows.

What you can trust

Recognising silent pain isn't about becoming a vet at home. It's about being the person who knows your dog best and saying to your vet, 'I've noticed these small changes, and I think he might be uncomfortable.' That sentence, backed by a simple checklist, opens the door to a treatment plan that could add months of comfortable, happy living to his life.

You're not being overly cautiousyou're being the advocate your stoic old mate deserves.

Frequently Asked Questions

My older dog still wags his tail and eats wellcould he really be in pain?
Yes, absolutely. Appetite and tail-wagging aren't reliable pain gauges. Many loving dogs maintain their normal greetings and food drive while quietly coping with chronic joint or back pain. It's the smaller shifts in mobility and behaviour that tell the true story.

What's the most common sign of hidden pain in large-breed seniors?
A gradual slowness to rise, especially after resting. You'll often see a 'stiff walk' that improves after a few steps. This is so common in older Labs and German Shepherds that owners mistake it for normal ageing, but it's frequently a signal of painful osteoarthritis.

Is a dog in pain if he chases the ball but limps afterwards?
Yes. Chasing and playing release feel-good chemicals that can temporarily mask pain. The limp afterwards tells you the joint isn't coping well with the activity. It's a classic pattern that your vet can help manage with rest periods, weight control, and pain relief.
