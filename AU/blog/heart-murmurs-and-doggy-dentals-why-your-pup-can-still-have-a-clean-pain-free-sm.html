Heart Murmurs and Doggy Dentals: Why Your Pup Can Still Have a Clean, Pain-Free Smile

心脏护理 · Published July 29, 2026 · 5 min read

---

G’day, Aussie dog lovers. If you’ve ever been told your furry mate has a heart murmur, you might feel your stomach drop at the mention of a dental clean. It’s a worry we hear all the time at the park: “Can my dog with a dicky ticker really go under anaesthetic, just for teeth?” The short answer is yes—with the right preparation, most dogs with stable heart disease can safely have that much-needed dental work. In fact, putting it off can cause more harm than good.

Let’s walk through what modern vet medicine actually says, so you can make decisions with confidence (and maybe a biscuit for the patient).

The Big Myth: “A Heart Murmur Means No Anaesthetic”

A lot of the fear comes from outdated info. A 2017 study in the Journal of the American Animal Hospital Association compared 100 dogs with heart disease to 100 healthy dogs, all having routine dental surgery at a teaching hospital. Both groups had the same type of monitoring and tailored anaesthetic plans. The result? The heart-disease dogs didn’t have significantly more complications. Not more scary drops in blood pressure, not more arrhythmias. In other words, when the vet team is careful, the risk isn’t as high as we once thought.

Here in Australia, that’s a real relief—especially for owners of Cavvies, Dachshunds, Poms, and other small breeds prone to that classic mitral valve murmur. No one wants their best mate living with a sore, infected mouth because of myths.

Why Dental Disease Actually Hurts a Dodgy Heart

Skipping a dental might feel like you’re protecting your dog, but rotten teeth and gums are a hidden enemy. The bacteria from severe tartar and gum infections can slip into the bloodstream and stress the heart even more. You might notice your dog panting at night, coughing after a run, or just seeming flat. Often we blame the heart alone, but the real culprit might be grotty teeth making everything worse. Cleaning them up under safe conditions often sees that nighttime cough ease right off.

Before the Big Day: What Your Vet Should Check

If your dog is over 7, or any age with a known murmur, your vet shouldn’t just “listen and guess”. Here’s your actionable checklist to chat about at your next visit:

  • NT-proBNP blood test: A simple blood draw that measures heart stretch. It helps catch hidden trouble before anaesthetic unmasks it.
  • Heart ultrasound (echocardiogram): Not every surgery needs a specialist, but an echo gives a clear picture of how well the heart is pumping, so the team can pick the gentlest drugs.
  • Stabilise any heart failure first: If your dog has fluid in the lungs or a weak heartbeat, medications like diuretics might need tweaking for a week or two before the dental.

Many local vets—from Brisbane to Bunbury—are fully capable of doing this work. The same study found that over 80% of referrals to specialist centres for heart-dog dentals were driven by vet anxiety rather than real risk. So don’t be surprised if your regular clinic can handle it confidently after proper planning.

A Gentle Anaesthetic Plan for a Tender Ticker

You don’t need to memorise this, but knowing the language helps you feel part of the team. For a heart patient, the vet might say something like:

> “We’ll use butorphanol for calming, propofol to drift off, and either isoflurane or sevoflurane gas to stay asleep. We’ll keep the gas low to avoid weakening the heart, and maybe add a constant drip of lignocaine to keep the rhythm steady.”

Throughout the procedure, they should monitor ECG, blood pressure, oxygen levels, and breathing like a hawk. That’s the standard of care—no cutting corners.

Real Story: A 12-Year-Old Pom with a Big Heart Issue and a Big Smile

A little fluffball called Teddy, a 12-year-old Pomeranian from Geelong, came in with breath that could knock a shearer sideways. His teeth were caked in brown gunk, and he had a grade 4 out of 6 heart murmur from mitral valve disease. His owner was terrified of anaesthesia and almost walked away. But after an echo showed Teddy’s heart was compensating okay, the vet team went ahead with the gentle plan. They cleaned every tooth, pulled two wobbly ones that were causing pain, and Teddy woke up smoothly. Two weeks later, his cough calmed down and his mouth felt so much better.

Home Care After the Dental: The Sodium Slip-Up to Avoid

Many heart dogs are on a low-sodium diet to stop fluid building up. A common mistake is bringing them home and offering a “special” treat or their old high-salt biscuits after a stressful day. That spike in sodium can mess with blood pressure and push a fragile heart into trouble. For the first 48 hours post-dental, stick strictly to their prescription cardiac food. Offer small amounts of water frequently but don’t let them gulp a whole bowl at once. Keep things peaceful—no zoomies, no excited visitors.

Can Supplements Help Before a Dental?

If your dog has been diagnosed with dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), especially if it’s the type linked to low taurine, your vet might suggest a 4-week lead-in with taurine (usually 30 mg/kg twice daily) and L-carnitine. This isn’t a cure, but it can support the heart muscle a bit, making the anaesthetic ride smoother. Never start supplements on your own—always under guidance and after a proper heart scan.

The Bottom Line for Your Mutt

You love them to bits, so it’s natural to worry. But skipping a necessary dental because of a murmur often means trading a small, managed risk for a bigger, slow-burn problem (like kidney strain from chronic infection). Talk to your vet about that NT-proBNP test, ask if your local clinic can do the procedure with heart monitoring, and don’t let old fears rob your dog of a pain-free gob.

Next time you’re at the dog park, you can be the one calming a worried mate: “Nah, my dog had it done. They just used heart-friendly gear and he was back to stealing socks by dinner.”

Here’s to clean teeth and wagging tails.
