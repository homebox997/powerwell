When to Worry About Stiffness in Your Senior Dog

July 27, 2026 · 6 min read

As our beloved dogs enter their golden years, it's natural to notice them slowing down a little. That extra nap in the sun, the slightly more deliberate pace on walks, or a moment of hesitation before jumping onto the sofa can all be part of normal ageing. But how do you know when stiffness crosses the line from 'just getting older' to a sign that needs veterinary attention? This is a question every Australian senior dog owner faces. The key is to distinguish between the gentle slowing of age and the concerning signals of pain, injury, or disease. In this article, we'll explore what's normal, what's not, and how you can support your faithful friend through their senior years with confidence and care.

Normal Age-Related Slowing vs Concerning Stiffness

It's completely normal for an older dog to move a little more slowly, especially first thing in the morning or after a long rest. They might take a few steps to 'warm up' before they're moving comfortably. This is often due to mild, age-related changes in muscles and joints, similar to how we might feel a bit creaky after a good night's sleep. However, concerning stiffness is different. It's persistent, gets worse over time, and often comes with other signs like reluctance to move, yelping when touched, or a change in their usual behaviour. If your dog seems stiff for more than a few minutes after getting up, or if the stiffness doesn't improve with gentle movement, it's time to take note.

The Range of Possible Causes

Stiffness in senior dogs isn't always just arthritis. While osteoarthritis is a common culprit, there are many other conditions that can cause mobility issues. Understanding the possibilities helps you have a more informed conversation with your vet.

Osteoarthritis

This is the most frequent cause of chronic stiffness in older dogs. It's a degenerative joint disease where the cartilage that cushions the joints wears down over time. This leads to pain, inflammation, and reduced mobility. It often affects weight-bearing joints like hips, knees, elbows, and shoulders.

Hip Dysplasia

Hip dysplasia is a developmental condition where the hip joint doesn't fit together properly. While it often starts in puppyhood, many dogs don't show signs until they are older, when arthritis sets in. A dog with hip dysplasia may have a 'bunny-hopping' gait, difficulty rising, or a swaying motion in their hindquarters. Interestingly, a dog with hip dysplasia might not always seem painfulthey can have a non-painful limp that is purely mechanical. This is why any persistent change in gait deserves a vet check.

Ligament Injuries

Injuries to the cranial cruciate ligament (similar to the ACL in humans) are common in dogs, especially as they age. This often causes a sudden onset of lameness in a hind leg, but it can also develop gradually. Your dog might hold their leg up, or you might notice them 'toe-touching'just barely putting weight on the affected leg. If left untreated, this can lead to long-term arthritis.

Spinal Issues

Problems like intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) or spondylosis (a degenerative condition of the spine) can cause stiffness, pain, and even weakness in the back or neck. Early signs of cervical spondylosis include a dull ache and stiffness in the neck, with reduced range of motion. As it progresses, it can press on nerves, leading to more serious symptoms. A dog with spinal issues might be reluctant to lower their head to eat, or they might walk with a stilted, uncoordinated gait.

Less Common Causes

While less frequent, stiffness can also be caused by infections like septic arthritis (a joint infection) or osteomyelitis (a bone infection). These conditions are serious and often come with red-flag signs like fever, swelling, and a refusal to walk. Other possibilities include autoimmune diseases or even bone cancer (osteosarcoma), which can cause a persistent, often painless limp that must be investigated.

Red-Flag Signs That Need Prompt Veterinary Attention

Some signs are not just 'wait and see'. If your senior dog shows any of the following, you should contact your veterinarian immediately or seek emergency care:

  • Fever: A temperature above 38.5C (101.3F), especially if combined with limping or refusal to walk.
  • Refusal to put weight on a leg: If your dog is holding a leg up completely and won't bear any weight on it.
  • Sudden, severe lameness: Especially after a known injury or fall.
  • Heat and swelling around a joint: This can indicate infection or inflammation.
  • Lethargy and loss of appetite: Your dog seems generally unwell, 'toxic', or depressed.
  • Yelping or crying when touched: This suggests significant pain.
  • Any limp that persists for more than a day or two: Even if it seems mild.

A common myth is that a limp without pain is not serious. This is false. A non-painful limp can be caused by serious mechanical issues like hip dysplasia, leg length differences, or early-stage bone cancer. Always get it checked.

What a Veterinary Examination Involves

When you bring your dog to the vet for stiffness or limping, the examination will be thorough. The vet will start by watching your dog walk and trot to assess their gait. They'll then do a hands-on orthopaedic exam, feeling each joint for heat, swelling, and range of motion. They may also perform a neurological exam to check for spinal issues. Depending on what they find, they might recommend:

  • X-rays: To look at bone structure, joint spaces, and signs of arthritis or dysplasia.
  • Blood tests: To check for infection, inflammation, or other systemic conditions.
  • Joint aspiration: If they suspect an infection like septic arthritis, they may take a sample of fluid from the joint for testing.
  • Advanced imaging: In some cases, an MRI or CT scan may be needed for a detailed view of soft tissues or the spine.

Never give your dog human pain relievers like ibuprofen or paracetamol. These are highly toxic to dogs and can cause severe harm. Your vet can prescribe safe, veterinary-specific pain relief and anti-inflammatory medications.

Practical Home Support for Your Senior Dog

While your vet manages the medical side, there is a lot you can do at home to make your dog more comfortable and support their mobility. Here are some practical, Australian-friendly tips:

Weight Management

Keeping your dog at a healthy weight is one of the most powerful things you can do. Extra pounds put enormous stress on ageing joints. Talk to your vet about an ideal weight and a suitable diet. Even a small amount of weight loss can make a big difference to your dog's comfort and energy levels.

Gentle, Low-Impact Exercise

Regular, gentle movement helps maintain muscle strength and joint flexibility. Short, frequent walks on soft surfaces like grass or sand are ideal. Swimming is excellent if your dog enjoys itit builds muscle without jarring the joints. Avoid high-impact activities like jumping, running on hard surfaces, or rough play. Listen to your dog; if they seem tired or stiff, it's time to rest.

Non-Slip Flooring

Slippery floors are a major hazard for senior dogs. A simple slip can cause injury or make them fearful of moving. Place non-slip mats or runners on tiles, wooden floors, and linoleum. You can also use dog booties with non-slip soles for extra grip.

Supportive Bedding

A good quality orthopaedic bed can make a world of difference. Look for thick, supportive foam that cushions joints and provides warmth. Memory foam beds are excellent. Place the bed in a warm, draft-free spot away from busy traffic areas.

Ramps and Steps

Help your dog avoid jumping on and off furniture, beds, or into the car. A sturdy ramp or a set of pet steps can reduce the impact on their joints. For cars, a telescopic ramp is a great investment. Make sure the ramp has a non-slip surface.

Joint Supplements

Supplements like glucosamine and chondroitin can support joint health. The typical dosage for glucosamine is 20-30 mg per pound of body weight per day, divided into two doses. For example, a 25 kg dog (about 55 lbs) might receive 500-750 mg twice daily. Always use a product formulated for pets, as human supplements may contain harmful ingredients like xylitol. It can take 4-6 weeks to see improvement, and you should always consult your vet before starting any supplement.

Closing Thoughts

Watching your senior dog slow down can be bittersweet, but you have the power to make their golden years comfortable and happy. By staying observant, knowing the red flags, and working closely with your veterinarian, you can address stiffness early and keep your best friend moving with dignity and joy. Remember, you are their advocate. If something doesn't seem right, trust your instincts and seek professional advice. Your loyal companion deserves nothing less.

Always consult your veterinarian before making any changes to your dog's care routine, especially regarding medication, supplements, or exercise. Every dog is unique, and your vet can provide the best advice for your senior dog's specific needs.
