Is Your Senior Dog Drinking More? Early Kidney Disease Signs

July 27, 2026 · 5 min read

Has your older dog started lapping up water like never before, asking to go outside more often, or even having accidents indoors? Many Australian pet parents dismiss this as just a normal part of ageing. But the truth is, a sudden increase in thirst and urination is one of the earliest and most important clues that your dogs kidneys may be struggling. In fact, research shows that more than one in three dogs aged eight years and older has some degree of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Understanding these early signs and knowing what to do can make a world of difference to your dogs comfort and longevity. Lets explore whats happening inside your senior dogs body and how you can take proactive steps to protect their kidney health.

Why Do Senior Dogs Drink More Water?

Its easy to assume that older dogs simply get thirstier as they age. But when a dog starts drinking noticeably more water (a condition called polydipsia) and urinating more frequently or in larger amounts (polyuria), its often a sign that the kidneys are no longer concentrating urine as effectively. Healthy kidneys filter waste from the blood and reabsorb water to keep the body hydrated. As kidney function declines, the tiny filters called nephrons become damaged and lose their ability to concentrate urine. This means your dog loses more water in their urine, so they need to drink more to stay hydrated. While other conditions like diabetes or Cushings disease can also cause these symptoms, CKD is one of the most common culprits in senior dogs.

What Increased Thirst and Urination Can Mean

When your dogs kidneys lose concentrating ability, the urine becomes dilute. You might notice they are urinating more frequently, producing larger puddles, or having accidents indoors, especially overnight. This is often the very first sign of early chronic kidney disease. Many owners mistakenly think their dog is just getting old or has a weak bladder, but its actually a signal that the kidneys are working harder to eliminate waste. If left unchecked, the condition can progress silently. Other early signs to watch for include a gradual loss of appetite, weight loss, and a drop in energy levels. Some dogs may also develop a dull coat or show subtle changes in behaviour.

Other Early Signs to Watch For

Beyond increased thirst and urination, keep an eye out for these early indicators:

  • Weight loss despite a normal or even increased appetite
  • Reduced interest in food or picky eating
  • Lethargy or sleeping more than usual
  • Dull, dry coat or flaky skin
  • Bad breath that has an ammonia-like odour (this can indicate advanced disease)

Remember, these signs can be very subtle at first. Many dogs with early CKD seem perfectly fine in other ways, which is why routine health checks are so important.

When to Worry and What Owners Should Do

If you notice your senior dog drinking more than usual or needing to urinate more often, its time to book a vet visit. Dont wait for other symptoms to appear. Your veterinarian will start with a thorough physical exam and recommend blood and urine tests. Traditional blood tests measure creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), but these only become elevated when around 75% of kidney function is lost. Fortunately, there is a much more sensitive marker called SDMA (symmetric dimethylarginine), which can detect a decline in kidney function as early as 25-40%. SDMA is not affected by muscle mass, making it especially useful for older or thin dogs. Urine tests check for protein loss and urine concentration, which can also indicate early damage.

Why Early Detection Matters

Finding CKD early gives you the best chance to slow its progression. Once diagnosed, your vet can recommend a management plan that often begins with a therapeutic kidney diet. These prescription diets are specially formulated to be lower in protein, phosphorus, and sodium, while being enriched with omega-3 fatty acids to reduce inflammation. Early intervention can also include medications to control blood pressure (around 60% of dogs with CKD have hypertension) and phosphorus binders to help maintain normal blood phosphorus levels. In some cases, your vet may suggest a newer biomarker called TMAO, which can detect kidney stress even earlier than traditional tests. By acting early, many dogs can enjoy months or even years of good quality life.

What a Diagnosis Might Look Like

Consider the story of a 12-year-old Labrador who started needing to go out multiple times each night and occasionally wetting indoors. Blood tests revealed elevated creatinine and SDMA, confirming stage 2 CKD. With a switch to a kidney diet, a phosphorus binder, and a blood pressure medication, his night-time accidents reduced significantly within three months. His energy improved, and he remained stable for over a year. For smaller dogs, early detection can be even more impactful. An 8-year-old Chihuahua with a borderline SDMA level and normal creatinine was started on a renal care diet and had her high-phosphorus treats removed. Six months later, her SDMA had returned to normal and her urine protein had cleared. These real-world examples show that early action can truly change the course of the disease.

Your Role in Supporting Kidney Health

As a pet parent, you can be your dogs best advocate. Keep a simple log of how much water your dog drinks and how often they urinate. Note any changes in appetite, weight, or energy. Schedule annual wellness exams that include blood work, urine analysis, and blood pressure checks from age seven onwards. Dont forget dental care bacteria from periodontal disease can travel through the bloodstream and damage the kidneys, so regular dental cleanings and home tooth brushing are important preventive measures. Maintaining a healthy weight and providing moderate exercise also support overall metabolic health.

A Final Word

Noticing your senior dog drinking more water or needing more toilet breaks can be worrying, but its also an opportunity. By recognising these early signs and working closely with your veterinarian, you can take meaningful steps to support your dogs kidneys and maintain their quality of life. Early detection truly is the key. Always consult your vet if you have any concerns they are your trusted partner in keeping your best friend happy and healthy for as long as possible.
