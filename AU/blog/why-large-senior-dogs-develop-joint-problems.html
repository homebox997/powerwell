Why Large Senior Dogs Develop Joint Problems

August 03, 2026 · 8 min read · PawWell Editorial

Why Large Senior Dogs Develop Joint Problems

Quick answer: A large frame places greater forces through weight-bearing joints, but size is only one part of the story. Genetics and joint development, past injuries, excess body fat, activity history and age can combine over many years. Joint pain is common in older large dogs, but it should not be written off as “just getting old.”

You may first notice it in a small pause: your Golden Retriever looks at the back step before climbing it, your German Shepherd circles twice before lying down, or your Rottweiler walks happily at first but trails behind on the way home. Dogs often adapt quietly. They take shorter strides, avoid slippery tiles or stop asking to jump into the car long before they develop an obvious limp.

More weight passes through a larger frame

Standing, turning, running and getting up all send force through joints. A large dog naturally carries more mass than a small dog, so sound joint shape, stable ligaments and well-conditioned muscles matter. This does not mean every large dog will develop arthritis. It means that developmental problems or extra body fat can have a bigger practical effect when more load is involved.

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a long-term joint disease involving the whole joint, not simply cartilage “wearing out.” Inflammation, changes to bone and joint tissues, reduced movement and pain can reinforce one another. It can develop after another joint problem or injury, and it may affect more than one joint.

Five factors that often overlap

1. Joint development and inherited risk

Hip dysplasia and elbow dysplasia are examples of developmental orthopaedic conditions seen in large breeds. The joint does not develop or fit as well as it should, changing how force is distributed. Genetics are important, while growth, nutrition, body condition and activity can influence how disease is expressed. Developmental disease may begin early even if discomfort becomes obvious only years later.

2. Body weight and body condition

A few extra kilograms matter on every step. Excess fat adds mechanical load and is also biologically active tissue linked with inflammation. Thick coats and a naturally broad build can make gradual weight gain difficult to judge, so ask your vet for a body condition score rather than relying only on the number on the scale.

3. Old injuries

A ligament injury, fracture, joint infection or surgery can change joint mechanics. The dog may look fully recovered while slower changes continue inside the joint. Tell your vet about old injuries even if they happened before you adopted the dog or many years ago.

4. Age and muscle loss

Older age is associated with higher OA risk, but age is not the same thing as pain. Senior dogs may also lose muscle, which can reduce joint support and make rising or balancing harder. A dog can have both arthritis and age-related muscle loss, so care should focus on comfort, safe movement and maintaining useful strength—not just “doing less.”

5. Activity history

Movement is essential for fitness and muscle, but injuries and poorly matched activity can contribute to joint problems. Weekend bursts of ball chasing after five quiet days are different from regular, controlled exercise. The right level depends on the dog, the affected joints and current pain; there is no universal daily step target.

These changes can also come from spinal, neurological, heart or other health problems. A clear video taken on a non-slip surface can help your vet see what happens at home, where many dogs move more naturally than in a clinic.

Arrange urgent veterinary care if your dog suddenly cannot stand, cries out with severe pain, drags a limb, has a visibly deformed or rapidly swelling joint, or deteriorates quickly. Keep movement to a minimum and do not give human pain medicines.

Small actions that protect comfort now

  • Place non-slip runners across polished floors and on the route to water and the garden.
  • Keep nails and paw fur managed so the feet can grip more securely.
  • Use a supportive bed in an easy-to-reach, draught-free spot.
  • Choose regular, controlled walks over sudden high-impact sessions until your vet advises what suits your dog.
  • Ask for both body condition and muscle condition to be checked at senior visits.

Do not force a painful joint through stretches found online. Do not start human pain relief, leftover veterinary medicine or a supplement mix without checking with your vet. Older dogs may have kidney, stomach, liver or heart considerations that change what is safe.

What a vet may assess

Your vet will usually ask what has changed, watch the dog move and examine the joints, muscles, spine and nervous system. They may recommend X-rays or other tests when the findings call for them. X-rays can show structural changes, but the amount seen on an image does not always match the amount of pain a dog experiences. Your observations of sleep, stairs, play, toileting and recovery after walks remain important.

The useful way to think about risk: size supplies the frame, but it does not act alone. Joint development, weight, old injuries, muscle and age meet in the individual dog. The earlier you notice a change in everyday choices, the sooner you and your vet can build a plan around comfortable movement.

Frequently asked questions

Is arthritis just a normal part of a large dog getting old?

No. Osteoarthritis becomes more common with age, but pain and reduced mobility should not be dismissed as normal ageing. A veterinary assessment can look for arthritis and other causes of slowing down.

Why does excess weight matter so much for joints?

Extra weight increases the load on weight-bearing joints, and fat tissue also contributes to inflammatory activity in the body. A safe weight plan should protect muscle as well as reduce excess fat.

Can an old injury cause arthritis years later?

Yes. Previous ligament damage, fracture, joint infection or abnormal joint development can alter how forces move through a joint and may contribute to osteoarthritis later.

Should a stiff senior dog rest completely?

Complete rest is not automatically the best choice for long-term joint health. Activity needs to be adjusted to the dog’s condition, and sudden or severe pain should be assessed before an exercise plan is changed.
