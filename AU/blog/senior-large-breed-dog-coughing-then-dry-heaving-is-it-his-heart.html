Senior Large-Breed Dog Coughing and Dry Heaving: Could It Be His Heart?

August 01, 2026 · AgedPawWell

Hearing your older dog cough and then retch as though something is stuck in his throat can be unsettling. Sometimes nothing comes up. At other times, you may see a little clear foam or saliva.

It is natural to wonder whether an ageing heart is responsible—particularly if your dog is a Doberman, Boxer, Great Dane, Irish Wolfhound or another breed associated with certain heart conditions.

Heart disease is one possibility, but coughing followed by gagging is not specific to the heart. Airway disease, infection, laryngeal paralysis, throat irritation and gastrointestinal emergencies can produce a similar pattern. The most useful first step is to look beyond the sound of the cough and assess how your dog is breathing and behaving.

Why Does a Cough Sometimes End in a Gag?

A strong cough can stimulate the throat and trigger a retching movement. To an owner, it may look as though the dog is trying to vomit, even when the episode began in the airways rather than the stomach.

Try to notice the sequence:

  • A cough followed by a gag may point towards airway or respiratory irritation.
  • Repeated attempts to vomit with nothing coming up—especially with drooling, restlessness or a swollen abdomen—may indicate gastric dilatation-volvulus, commonly called GDV or bloat.
  • A harsh noise when breathing in may suggest an upper-airway problem.
  • Faster or more difficult breathing at rest raises concern about the lungs, heart or another serious condition.

A short video of an episode can be extremely helpful to your veterinarian.

Could the Heart Be Responsible?

Yes, but a cough alone cannot diagnose heart disease or heart failure.

Some large and giant breeds are at increased risk of dilated cardiomyopathy, or DCM. In DCM, the heart muscle becomes weak and the heart chambers enlarge. Some affected dogs develop abnormal heart rhythms; others eventually develop congestive heart failure.

When left-sided congestive heart failure causes fluid to accumulate in the lungs, dogs commonly develop faster or more difficult breathing. Coughing, reduced stamina, restlessness and difficulty settling may also occur.

However, many coughing dogs—even dogs that also have a heart murmur—are not in heart failure. Chronic airway or lung disease can produce very similar symptoms. A veterinarian may need chest X-rays and other tests to determine whether fluid is actually present in the lungs.

Breathing Rate May Tell You More Than the Cough

Your dog's sleeping respiratory rate can provide useful information.

Wait until your dog is fully asleep—not panting, dreaming actively or recovering from exercise. Count each rise and fall of the chest as one breath for a full 60 seconds.

In many healthy dogs, the sleeping rate is below 30 breaths per minute. One isolated higher count does not confirm heart failure, but a rate that is repeatedly above your dog's normal level—particularly above 30—or continues to rise deserves veterinary advice.

Breathing effort matters just as much as the number. Seek urgent help if your dog:

  • Uses the abdominal muscles strongly to breathe
  • Cannot lie down or settle
  • Stretches the neck forward to breathe
  • Breathes with the mouth open while resting
  • Has grey, blue or very pale gums
  • Appears panicked, weak or close to collapsing

Do not spend several minutes counting breaths if your dog is already struggling. Go directly to an emergency veterinary clinic.

Other Possible Causes

Laryngeal paralysis

Laryngeal paralysis is an important consideration in older large-breed dogs, particularly Labradors.

The larynx normally opens to allow air into the windpipe. When it does not open properly, a dog may develop noisy or raspy breathing, a changed bark, coughing or gagging while eating and reduced tolerance of heat or exercise.

Symptoms often become worse during hot weather, excitement or strenuous activity. Severe breathing difficulty is an emergency because overheating and airway swelling can rapidly worsen the obstruction.

Infectious respiratory disease

Canine infectious respiratory disease—often called kennel cough—commonly causes a dry, hacking or honking cough that ends in a gag. Exposure can occur at boarding facilities, grooming salons, dog parks, training classes or other places where dogs gather.

Some dogs remain bright and continue eating, while others develop nasal discharge, fever, lethargy or reduced appetite. Senior dogs and dogs with existing heart or lung disease may become more unwell and should be assessed promptly.

Keep a coughing dog away from other dogs until your veterinarian advises that it is safe.

Chronic bronchitis or other lung disease

Inflammation of the lower airways can cause a persistent cough and reduced exercise tolerance. Pneumonia, lung masses and other respiratory conditions may also resemble heart disease.

The sound of a cough is not enough to reliably separate bronchitis, pneumonia and heart failure. Listening to the chest is useful, but imaging is often necessary.

Heartworm disease

Heartworm is transmitted by mosquitoes and remains a concern in parts of Australia, with risk varying by region and travel history. Infected dogs may initially show no symptoms. When illness develops, coughing, reduced stamina and breathing difficulty can occur.

Tell your veterinarian if prevention has been inconsistent or your dog has lived in or travelled through a heartworm-risk area. Testing and treatment decisions depend on the dog's history and clinical findings.

Throat irritation or a foreign object

Grass seeds, plant material, a fragment of food or another foreign object may irritate the throat or airway. Sudden coughing, repeated swallowing, pawing at the mouth or distress after chewing or walking through long grass should be taken seriously.

Do not reach blindly into the back of your dog's throat. You could push an object farther in or be bitten by a frightened dog.

Gastric dilatation-volvulus

GDV is different from a cough that happens to end in a gag. It typically causes repeated, unproductive attempts to vomit. Affected dogs may drool, pace, look anxiously at the abdomen or develop a tight, enlarging belly.

Large, deep-chested dogs have a higher risk, but the condition can affect other dogs too. GDV is immediately life-threatening and requires emergency treatment. Do not wait to see whether the dog settles, and do not offer food, water or home medication on the way to the clinic.

Warning Signs That Need Emergency Care

Contact an emergency veterinary clinic immediately if your dog has:

  • Laboured or open-mouth breathing at rest
  • Grey, blue or very pale gums
  • Collapse, fainting or profound weakness
  • Repeated unproductive retching
  • A swollen, tense or painful abdomen
  • Severe restlessness combined with drooling
  • Coughing accompanied by obvious respiratory distress
  • Rapidly worsening symptoms
  • A suspected airway obstruction

Fainting can be associated with an abnormal heart rhythm, but seizures and other conditions may look similar. Any collapse in a senior dog deserves prompt veterinary assessment.

What You Can Safely Do at Home

If your dog is currently comfortable and breathing normally:

  • Record a video. Capture the cough, gag and breathing sound if you can do so without stressing your dog.
  • Count the sleeping respiratory rate. Record the result and time rather than relying on memory.
  • Write down the pattern. Note whether episodes occur after drinking, eating, exercise, excitement, collar pressure or lying down.
  • Keep activity gentle. Avoid strenuous walks, heat and excitement until you have spoken with your veterinarian.
  • Use a harness for necessary toilet trips. This may reduce pressure on an irritated throat, but it is not a treatment for breathing disease.
  • Call your veterinary clinic. A new cough in a senior dog should be assessed, especially when it keeps returning, affects sleep or occurs with reduced stamina.

Do not give human cough medicine, leftover antibiotics, diuretics or heart medication. These products may be dangerous, mask important symptoms or worsen the wrong condition.

Do not automatically restrict salt, change diets or add taurine, L-carnitine or other supplements. Nutritional changes should be based on the diagnosis and the dog's complete diet.

What the Veterinarian May Recommend

The examination may begin with listening to the heart and lungs, checking gum colour, measuring the breathing rate and feeling the throat and abdomen.

Depending on the findings, testing may include:

  • Chest X-rays to assess the lungs, airways and heart silhouette
  • Blood tests to look for infection, anaemia and organ problems
  • Heartworm testing where relevant
  • An electrocardiogram to check the heart rhythm
  • An echocardiogram to examine heart structure and function
  • NT-proBNP or another cardiac biomarker as part of the broader assessment
  • Airway examination or additional imaging when laryngeal or respiratory disease is suspected

An echocardiogram can identify structural heart disease, but it does not by itself prove that a cough is caused by fluid in the lungs. Your veterinarian interprets the examination, X-rays and other findings together.

If Heart Disease Is Diagnosed

Heart disease does not have one standard treatment. Medication depends on the exact condition, its stage, whether fluid is present and whether an abnormal rhythm has developed.

Some dogs may be prescribed a diuretic, pimobendan or other cardiac medication. Others with early or different forms of heart disease may need another plan or simply regular monitoring.

Never start, stop or change the dose of heart medication without veterinary direction. A treatment that is helpful for confirmed congestive heart failure may be inappropriate for a dog whose cough comes from the airways.

With an accurate diagnosis and appropriate care, many senior dogs can continue enjoying comfortable walks, meals and family time.

Listen to the Breathing, Not Just the Cough

A cough followed by dry heaving is a reason to pay attention, but it is not proof of heart failure. In many dogs, the cause lies in the throat, airways or lungs. In others, heart disease may be contributing. Repeated unproductive retching with abdominal swelling is a different emergency altogether.

Watch how your dog breathes at rest, record what you see and seek veterinary care based on the whole picture. You do not need to diagnose the problem at home. Noticing the change and getting the right assessment is already an important act of care.
