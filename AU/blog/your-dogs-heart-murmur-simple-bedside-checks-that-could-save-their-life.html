Your Dog's Heart Murmur: Simple Bedside Checks That Could Save Their Life

心脏护理 · Published July 29, 2026 · 7 min read

---

G’day, fellow dog lover. If your vet recently mentioned a heart murmur at the annual check-up, your ears might still be ringing a little. It’s completely normal to feel worried, but here’s the good news: you can do more than just watch and wait. There’s a dead-easy, completely free tool you already have at home—and it’s far more powerful than you’d think. Let’s walk through what that leaky valve really means and how a few quiet moments on the couch can give you the upper hand.

Why the vet saying ‘murmur’ isn’t the end of the story

A heart murmur is simply the sound your vet hears when blood flows through a valve that isn’t closing perfectly. In older small dogs—especially Cavaliers, Dachshunds and Miniature Poodles—this often comes down to some age-related wear on the mitral valve (vets call it MMVD, short for mitral valve disease). Think of it like a door that’s developed a tiny gap, letting a little blood backtrack. Many dogs live comfortably with a murmur for years without a single hiccup.

But not all murmurs are equal. The volume of the murmur doesn’t tell you how advanced the problem is. A grade 3 murmur can live alongside a completely normal‑sized heart, while a softer murmur can hide significant stretching. The only way to know what’s really happening inside is with an ultrasound—and a few clever observations you can make at home.

Counting your dog’s sleeping breaths: your secret home weapon

When your best mate is properly asleep—not dreaming, not twitching, just deeply zonked—their breathing tells the truest story. This is called the sleeping respiratory rate, and veterinary cardiologists swear by it. It’s the first number that starts to creep up if fluid is beginning to gather in the lungs, often before a cough even appears.

Here’s how to do it tonight:

  1. Wait until your dog is in a deep sleep. No dreaming, no wagging tails.
  2. Watch their chest rise and fall. One full rise-and-fall counts as a single breath.
  3. Set your phone timer for 15 seconds and count the breaths.
  4. Multiply by four to get the breaths per minute.

Write that number on the fridge. A normal sleeping rate is usually under 30 breaths per minute. If your dog’s number is consistently above 30–35 when completely relaxed—say, for a few nights in a row—ring your vet for a chat. On a stinking hot Queensland afternoon, a dog might pant faster, but when the air con is on and they’re snoring softly, the rate should come right down.

Danger signs that mean drop everything and go to the vet

When that leaky valve allows fluid to build up suddenly, it becomes an emergency. Trust your gut, but also watch for these clear red flags:

  • Breathing that’s open-mouthed and laboured while the dog is just lying still
  • A belly or chest that’s heaving with visible effort
  • Suddenly collapsing or fainting
  • A sleeping breathing rate that stays well above 40 breaths per minute, even on cool nights

If you see any of those, don’t wait for the morning. Head straight to your nearest emergency vet. Fluid on the lungs can escalate in hours, and quick action really does save lives.

The little dog breed that needs a special eye: Cavaliers

If you share your home with a Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, you’ve probably already heard they have a heart-shaped nose and an even bigger heart. Unfortunately, that comes with a genuine risk. Studies show Cavaliers are 20 to 30 times more likely to develop this valve disease than mixed-breed dogs, and almost all of them will have it by age ten. They also tend to develop it younger.

The silver lining? Responsible breeders across Australia are now using heart ultrasounds on their breeding dogs. This doesn’t wipe out the condition, but it can push back the age it appears. If you’re thinking of adding a Cavalier pup to your family, ask the breeder directly: “Do you have recent echocardiogram results for both parents?” It’s the single most powerful question you can ask.

A tablet that can buy precious extra time

A landmark study called the EPIC trial gave thousands of dog owners a reason to feel hopeful. Researchers found that dogs whose leaky valve had already caused the heart to stretch (a stage vets call B2) could delay the onset of actual heart failure by about 15 months simply by taking a medicine called pimobendan twice a day. That’s over a year of extra zoomies, snuggles and Saturday morning markets together.

Pimobendan helps the heart pump more efficiently and slows the harmful changes inside. The dose is worked out carefully for your dog’s weight—roughly 0.25–0.3 mg per kilogram every 12 hours—so it’s never a guess. This isn’t a pill for every dog with a murmur; it’s specifically for those whose heart has enlarged, and only your vet (often with the help of a cardiologist) can decide if it’s the right time to start.

When do fluid tablets come into the picture?

If heart failure does arrive, vets bring in medicines called diuretics to clear the extra fluid from the lungs. The most common one is frusemide (you’ll often hear it called furosemide). It works quickly to make breathing easier by helping the kidneys flush out water. For dogs whose symptoms are trickier to manage, a longer‑acting option called torsemide might be added or swapped in. Both are prescription‑only and need careful monitoring, so you’ll be working closely with your vet.

Sometimes the pressure backs up so much that it creates high blood pressure in the lungs—a condition called pulmonary hypertension. When that happens, a medicine called sildenafil can relax those lung vessels and take the load off the heart. In Australia, this is the same active ingredient as a well‑known human tablet, but your vet will prescribe the veterinary form at the right dose.

What about special foods or supplements?

A balanced diet matters, but no magic recipe can mend a worn valve. Watch out for any brand that promises a “heart‑healthy” food will stop or reverse mitral valve disease—it simply cannot. The most practical dietary move you can make is to keep salt low once your vet says the heart is starting to enlarge or failure is creeping in. That means skipping salty treats like leftover sausage sizzle, deli meats or chips, which can make fluid retention worse. Otherwise, a top‑quality, age‑appropriate Aussie dog food covers all the bases.

Two myths that worry owners unnecessarily

Myth 1: “The louder the murmur, the worse the disease.”

Not true. Vets grade murmurs from 1 to 6 based on loudness, but loudness alone doesn’t reveal how severe the leak is. A small leak can be surprisingly loud, and a big leak can sometimes sound quieter. The real decision‑maker is the ultrasound measurement of the heart chambers.

Myth 2: “Every time my dog with a murmur coughs, it’s heart failure.”

Small dogs prone to valve disease are also prone to other cough makers—collapsing windpipes, bronchitis, kennel cough or just seasonal irritation. A chest X‑ray is the only way to tell if that cough is coming from fluid in the lungs or an irritated airway. So don’t panic with every hack, but do get it checked if it hangs around.

Your 5‑minute plan for tonight

You don’t need fancy gadgets or a vet degree. All you need is a quiet night, a sleeping dog and your phone.

  • While your dog is deep asleep (not dreaming), count those chest rises for 15 seconds. Multiply by four.
  • Jot the number down. Do it a few nights in a row to build your pup’s “normal” baseline.
  • If the trend starts creeping over 30–35, or if you ever spot open‑mouthed breathing at rest, ring your vet.

That’s it. You’ve just put yourself in the driver’s seat of your best friend’s heart health. And remember, you’re not alone—your vet team is right there with you, and Australian cardiology specialists are world‑class. Together, you can catch problems early and keep those tails wagging for as long as possible.
