Dark or Bloody Urine in a Large Dog: What It Could Mean and What to Do Tonight

August 01, 2026 · AgedPawWell

Noticing red, brown or unusually dark urine when your older dog goes outside can be frightening. It may be tempting to wait and see whether the next wee looks normal, especially if your dog still seems comfortable.

However, visible blood or an unexplained change in urine colour should always be discussed with a veterinarian. Sometimes the cause is a treatable bladder infection. In other cases, it may involve urinary stones, bleeding, toxin exposure, prostate disease or damage to the kidneys or muscles.

The colour alone cannot tell you what is wrong, but your dog's other symptoms can help you decide how urgently care is needed.

What Can Make a Dog's Urine Look Dark or Bloody?

Normal dog urine is generally clear and pale to medium yellow. Urine that looks pink, red, rust-coloured, brown or similar to tea may contain different substances:

  • Blood in the urine (haematuria): This may come from the kidneys, bladder, urethra, prostate or reproductive tract.
  • Haemoglobin: Red blood cells breaking down inside the body can release pigment that makes urine look red or brown.
  • Myoglobin: Serious muscle injury can release myoglobin and produce dark brown urine.
  • Bilirubin: Liver or gallbladder problems can sometimes make urine appear unusually dark yellow, orange or brown.
  • Highly concentrated urine: Dehydration can deepen the colour, although it should not be assumed to explain clearly red or blood-stained urine.

Grass, soil and evening light can also change how a puddle looks. If possible, look at a fresh sample in a clean, clear container—but do not delay veterinary care while trying to collect one.

Common Causes Your Vet May Consider

Urinary tract infection

Inflammation caused by a bacterial urinary infection can result in blood, frequent squatting, straining, accidents inside the house or a stronger-than-usual smell. Some dogs lick around the urinary opening or seem uncomfortable when passing urine.

If an infection reaches the kidneys, a dog may also become lethargic, lose interest in food, vomit, develop a fever or appear painful around the back or abdomen.

These symptoms cannot confirm an infection by themselves. A veterinarian may need a urinalysis and urine culture before deciding whether antibiotics are appropriate.

Bladder or urinary stones

Stones can irritate the lining of the urinary tract and cause pain or bleeding. You may notice repeated attempts to urinate, a weak stream, small amounts of urine or blood near the end of urination.

A stone can sometimes block the urethra, particularly in male dogs. A dog repeatedly trying to urinate but producing only drops—or nothing at all—needs emergency veterinary treatment.

Prostate disease

In an older, unneutered male dog, blood in the urine or blood-stained discharge may be connected to an enlarged or inflamed prostate. Prostate disease may also cause difficulty passing stool, abdominal discomfort, fever or stiffness.

Prostate problems are not limited to large dogs, and examination or imaging is usually needed to identify the cause.

Trauma

A fall, vehicle accident, hard collision or blow to the abdomen can damage the urinary tract or cause internal bleeding. A dog may initially appear relatively normal after an accident.

If dark or bloody urine follows any significant trauma, contact a veterinarian immediately, even if your dog is still walking around.

Toxins and medications

Rat bait, grapes, raisins, sultanas, antifreeze and some human medications can cause serious illness involving the kidneys, blood or clotting system. Human painkillers such as ibuprofen and naproxen can be especially dangerous to dogs.

Do not wait for more symptoms if toxin exposure is possible. Contact an emergency veterinarian or an animal poison service straight away. Do not make your dog vomit unless a veterinary professional specifically instructs you to do so.

Bleeding or red-blood-cell disorders

Clotting disorders, severe inflammation, immune-mediated disease and some infections can lead to bleeding or the destruction of red blood cells. In Australia, tick-borne disease may be relevant depending on where your dog lives or has travelled.

Look for pale or yellow gums, bruising, bleeding from the nose, marked weakness, rapid breathing or collapse. These signs require urgent veterinary attention.

Bladder, kidney or urinary tract tumours

Cancer is not the most common explanation for a single episode of discoloured urine, but it is one possibility—particularly when blood keeps returning or an older dog also strains, urinates frequently or loses weight.

Blood in the urine does not automatically mean cancer. Testing is needed before conclusions can be drawn.

Kidney disease or acute kidney injury

Kidney problems can affect urine production, but visible blood is not a reliable sign of ordinary chronic kidney disease by itself. Acute kidney injury, infection, stones, trauma and toxins may all involve the kidneys and require prompt assessment.

Vomiting, profound tiredness, loss of appetite, abdominal pain or a sudden reduction in urine production makes the situation more urgent.

What to Do Tonight

1. Watch your dog urinate

Check whether your dog produces a normal stream, only a few drops or no urine at all. Repeated straining without passing urine is an emergency.

Do not confuse attempts to urinate with constipation. If you are uncertain, it is safest to phone an emergency veterinary clinic.

2. Look at your dog's overall condition

Pay attention to breathing, energy, gum colour, comfort and ability to stand. Pale, white, yellow, grey or blue gums are concerning, especially when accompanied by weakness or rapid breathing.

Avoid repeatedly pressing the gums or performing multiple home tests if your dog is distressed. The priority is obtaining veterinary help.

3. Check for possible toxin exposure

Look for damaged medication packets, rat bait, spilled chemicals, missing grapes or raisins, and anything unusual your dog may have eaten.

Keep the packet or take a photograph of the label. The exact product, estimated amount and possible time of exposure can help the veterinarian determine treatment.

4. Offer water—but do not force it

If your dog is alert and not vomiting, leave fresh water available. Do not force water into the mouth and do not give homemade electrolyte mixtures.

Do not begin supplements, leftover antibiotics or human medication.

5. Collect a urine sample if it is easy and safe

If your dog can urinate normally, catch a midstream sample in a clean, shallow container. Transfer it to a clean, sealed container and note the collection time.

A fresh sample is best. If the clinic advises you to bring it later, refrigerate it and follow the clinic's instructions. Do not let sample collection delay an emergency visit.

6. Photograph the colour and record the symptoms

A photograph taken in good light may help show the veterinarian what you saw. Write down:

  • When the colour change started
  • How often your dog is trying to urinate
  • Whether a normal stream is produced
  • Any vomiting, weakness, pain or appetite change
  • Recent medications, injuries, travel or possible toxin exposure

When It Is an Emergency

Go to an emergency veterinary clinic or call one immediately if your dog:

  • Repeatedly strains but passes no urine or only a few drops
  • Collapses, cannot stand or becomes profoundly weak
  • Has pale, white, yellow, grey or blue gums
  • Has difficulty breathing
  • Develops a swollen, tense or painful abdomen
  • Cannot keep water down because of repeated vomiting
  • Passes a large amount of blood or blood clots
  • Has recently experienced significant trauma
  • May have eaten rat bait, grapes, raisins, human painkillers, antifreeze or another toxin
  • Shows bleeding from the nose, mouth or other parts of the body

If your dog is still passing urine and otherwise appears comfortable, contact your regular veterinarian promptly for advice and arrange an examination. Visible blood should not simply be monitored for several days, even if the next urination looks normal.

What the Veterinarian May Check

Depending on the symptoms, your veterinarian may recommend:

  • Physical examination
  • Urinalysis
  • Urine culture
  • Blood tests
  • Blood pressure measurement
  • X-rays or ultrasound
  • Clotting tests
  • Tests for infectious or immune-mediated disease

Urine colour alone cannot show whether the problem comes from the bladder, kidneys, prostate, blood or muscles. Testing helps avoid treating the wrong condition.

Can It Be Prevented?

Not every episode can be prevented, but a few habits can reduce avoidable risks:

  • Keep fresh water available at all times.
  • Store medications, chemicals and rat bait securely.
  • Never give human pain medication unless a veterinarian specifically directs it.
  • Arrange regular senior health examinations, including blood and urine testing when recommended.
  • Notice changes in urination frequency, effort and volume.
  • Follow a veterinarian's dietary recommendations if your dog has previously developed urinary stones or kidney disease.
  • Avoid changing to a low-protein or "kidney" diet solely because the urine looks dark. Different urinary conditions require different nutritional approaches, and the wrong diet may be unhelpful.

A Calm but Prompt Response

Dark or bloody urine is a sign to take seriously, but it does not automatically mean that your dog has cancer or irreversible kidney failure. Infections, stones and several other causes can often be managed more successfully when assessed early.

Your job tonight is not to diagnose the colour. It is to check whether your dog can urinate, watch for emergency warning signs, consider possible toxin exposure and contact a veterinary professional at the appropriate level of urgency.

Noticing the change and acting promptly is one of the most useful things you can do for your old mate.
