Pulmonary Hypertension in Dogs: Understanding Diagnosis and Recovery

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a serious condition where high blood pressure in the lungs strains the right side of the heart. This Australian guide explains the causes, symptoms, treatment options, and what to expect during recovery.

💡 Key Takeaway

Pulmonary hypertension is serious but increasingly manageable with modern medications. Early diagnosis and treating the underlying cause are key to the best outcomes. With appropriate therapy, many dogs experience significant improvement in breathing and quality of life, allowing them to enjoy months to years of comfortable living.

What is Pulmonary Hypertension?

To understand pulmonary hypertension, it helps to first understand how blood normally flows through your dog's body. After blood circulates through the body and returns to the heart, the right side of the heart pumps it through the lungs to pick up oxygen. This pulmonary circulation operates at much lower pressure than the rest of the body — the lungs present very little resistance to blood flow.

In pulmonary hypertension, something goes wrong with this system. The blood vessels in the lungs become narrowed, blocked, or damaged, causing the pressure to rise. This forces the right side of the heart to work much harder to push blood through. Over time, the right ventricle thickens and eventually weakens, leading to right-sided heart failure.

Think of it like trying to push water through a partially blocked hose — you need much more pressure to achieve the same flow. The heart compensates for a while, but eventually struggles to keep up.

⚠️ Why PH is Different from Other Heart Conditions

Pulmonary hypertension is not the same as "regular" heart disease. It specifically affects the lung blood vessels and right heart. The medications used to treat left-sided heart disease (like pimobendan) are not always appropriate for PH and can sometimes be harmful. This is why accurate diagnosis by a veterinary cardiologist is essential.

Causes and Underlying Conditions

Pulmonary hypertension rarely occurs on its own. It's usually a complication of another condition. Understanding the underlying cause is crucial because treatment must address both the PH and its source:

🫁 Left-Sided Heart Disease
The most common cause in dogs. When the left heart fails (from mitral valve disease or DCM), pressure backs up into the lungs, causing pulmonary venous hypertension. Treating the underlying heart disease is essential.
🦠 Respiratory Disease
Chronic bronchitis, interstitial lung disease, and severe tracheal collapse can damage lung vessels. In Australia, smoke from bushfires has also been implicated in respiratory complications in dogs.
🩸 Heartworm Disease
Heartworms physically block pulmonary vessels. Though preventable with monthly medication, heartworm still occurs in Australia, particularly in northern states. PH from heartworm can be severe and difficult to treat.
🧬 Pulmonary Thromboembolism
Blood clots lodging in the lung vessels. Can occur with Cushing's disease, immune-mediated haemolytic anaemia, or protein-losing kidney disease. Requires anticoagulation therapy alongside PH treatment.

Other, rarer causes include congenital heart defects (like PDA), pulmonary artery stenosis, and idiopathic PH where no underlying cause can be identified despite thorough investigation.

🐕🫁
Difficulty breathing, especially after mild exercise, is one of the hallmark signs of pulmonary hypertension. Dogs may seem winded after very short walks or reluctant to move at all.

Recognising the Symptoms

Symptoms of pulmonary hypertension often develop gradually, making them easy to dismiss as "just getting older" or "out of shape." Here's what to watch for:

Breathing Difficulties

  • Shortness of breath — laboured breathing, even at rest in severe cases
  • Exercise intolerance — tiring quickly, stopping to catch breath after minimal activity
  • Rapid breathing — elevated respiratory rate even when calm
  • Coughing — may or may not be present, depending on underlying cause

Other Signs

  • Fainting or collapsing — particularly after exercise or excitement (syncope)
  • Distended abdomen — fluid accumulation from right heart failure (ascites)
  • Blue-tinged gums — indicating low oxygen levels (cyanosis)
  • Weakness and lethargy — reluctance to move, sleeping more
  • Sudden death — in severe cases, PH can cause fatal arrhythmias

If your dog shows any of these signs, particularly exercise intolerance and breathing difficulties, seek veterinary attention promptly. Early diagnosis offers the best chance of effective management.

How PH is Diagnosed

Diagnosing pulmonary hypertension requires specialised testing, often by a veterinary cardiologist. Here's what you can expect:

Physical Examination and Auscultation

Your vet will listen for heart murmurs, abnormal lung sounds, and split heart sounds. A split S2 heart sound is particularly suggestive of PH. However, examination alone cannot confirm PH — further testing is essential.

Echocardiogram (Heart Ultrasound)

The most important diagnostic tool. An echo can estimate pulmonary artery pressure by measuring blood flow velocity and assessing right heart size and function. Right ventricular enlargement and flattening of the interventricular septum are key signs. Available at specialist practices across Australia.

Chest X-rays

Shows heart size, lung patterns, and can identify underlying lung disease or heartworm. Enlarged pulmonary arteries may be visible. X-rays help determine if respiratory disease is contributing to PH.

Blood Tests and Heartworm Testing

Essential to identify underlying causes. Heartworm antigen testing, complete blood count, biochemistry, and tests for Cushing's disease or blood clotting disorders may be recommended.

Cardiac Catheterisation

The gold standard for measuring pulmonary pressure directly, but rarely performed in dogs as it requires general anaesthesia and is invasive. Echocardiography is usually sufficient for diagnosis and monitoring.

Treatment Options Available in Australia

Treatment for pulmonary hypertension has two components: addressing the underlying cause (when possible) and managing the elevated pulmonary pressure itself:

💊 Sildenafil (Viagra/Revatio)
The most commonly prescribed medication for PH in dogs. It dilates pulmonary blood vessels, reducing pressure and improving blood flow. Available as compounded liquid or tablets. Significant improvement in breathing and exercise tolerance often seen within days.
💊 Tadalafil (Cialis)
Similar to sildenafil but longer-acting, allowing once or twice daily dosing. Sometimes used when sildenafil is ineffective or causes side effects. More expensive but more convenient.
💊 Pimobendan
Used when PH is secondary to left-sided heart disease. Strengthens heart contractions and dilates blood vessels. Not appropriate for all PH cases — must be prescribed by a cardiologist familiar with your dog's specific condition.
💊 Diuretics (Furosemide, Spironolactone)
Remove excess fluid when right heart failure is present. Reduce fluid in the abdomen (ascites) and help with breathing. Dosage adjusted based on response and kidney function.

Cost considerations in Australia: Sildenafil compounding typically costs $80–150 per month depending on dog size. Tadalafil is more expensive at $120–200 per month. These medications are not PBS-subsidised for veterinary use, but some pet insurance policies cover them.

Recovery and Long-Term Management

The outlook for dogs with pulmonary hypertension depends heavily on the underlying cause and how early treatment begins. Here's what you can generally expect:

📅 Recovery and Management Timeline
1

First Days of Treatment

If sildenafil or similar medications are effective, you may see improvement in breathing within 3–7 days. Your dog should seem more comfortable and willing to move around. Report any changes — positive or negative — to your vet.

2

Weeks 2–4

Medication doses are adjusted based on response. Repeat echocardiograms may be scheduled to assess improvement. Underlying conditions (heartworm, lung disease) receive targeted treatment. Activity should gradually increase if responding well.

3

Long-Term Management

PH is rarely "cured" — it's managed. Dogs typically remain on medication for life. Regular check-ups every 3–6 months monitor heart and lung function. Home monitoring of breathing rate and activity level is essential.

4

Quality of Life Considerations

Many dogs with well-managed PH enjoy good quality of life for months to years. Focus on comfortable living — short, gentle walks, avoiding heat and humidity, maintaining healthy weight, and minimising stress. Your vet can help assess quality of life at any stage.

Prognosis

Prognosis varies considerably:

  • PH from treatable causes (heartworm, some heart diseases) — prognosis can be good if the underlying cause is successfully addressed
  • PH from progressive conditions (severe heart disease, advanced lung disease) — management focuses on comfort and slowing progression
  • Idiopathic PH — variable; some dogs respond excellently to sildenafil, others have more limited response

The most important factors are early diagnosis, treating underlying causes, and regular monitoring. Dogs that respond well to initial therapy often enjoy meaningful extensions of good-quality life.
