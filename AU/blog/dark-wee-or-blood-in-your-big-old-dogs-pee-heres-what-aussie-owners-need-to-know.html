Senior Dog Kidney & Urinary Health

Dark Wee or Blood in Your Big Old Dogs Pee? Heres What Aussie Owners Need to Know

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 7 August 2026 · 6 min read

Vet Disclaimer: Content for education only. Please consult your vet for your pets individual health advice. If your dog shows urgent signs (cannot urinate, difficulty breathing, collapse, pale gums, seizures), contact a vet or emergency clinic immediately.
Quick answer: Blood in the urine (vets call it haematuria) in a large senior dog most often points to a urinary tract infection (UTI), bladder stones or crystals, a prostate problem in intact males, or early kidney stress. It can also be a burst polyp or post-play irritation. Some causes clear up with antibiotics; others need ongoing management. A vet visit with a urine sample and possibly bloods or an ultrasound is the safest next step.

You Spot It After a Beach Walk — Now What?

Youve just got home from a ripper beach walk, your old Labrador or German Shepherd is flopped on the cool tiles, and you notice a patch of dark, rusty-coloured wee on the grass. Maybe its pink-tinged, or you see tiny red specks. Your heart skips. You reckon its probably nothing — they just drank less today — but a nagging voice says, what if its serious?

Lets have a cuppa and yarn about what dark urine or blood in a big senior dogs wee really means. Ill cover the most common reasons (some are straightforward, some need quick action), how you can do a few simple checks at home tonight, and the one red-flag sign that means you drop everything and head to the vet.

Why Theres Blood: A Simple Breakdown

Think of the urinary system like a plumbing line. Blood can show up because of inflammation (like a UTI scratching the bladder lining), physical damage (stones rubbing), a growth, or a bleeding concern from kidney filtration. In large breeds, two things get a bit more common with age:

  • Bladder stones or crystals: Some mineral types just love big dogs — struvite stones often come with infections, while calcium oxalate stones can form for sneaky metabolic reasons. They scratch the bladder wall and make weeing uncomfortable.
  • Kidney changes: As large dogs age, kidney function can dip. Sometimes the first outward sign is darker, concentrated urine or a tiny amount of blood. Thats why we always chat about kidney health in senior dogs — catching it early gives you more options to slow things down.

In intact male dogs, an enlarged prostate can bleed into the urine too; thats less common if your mate is desexed, but its on the vets checklist.

Illustrative Scenario — Not a Real Patient

Illustrative scenario — not a real patient. Imagine Max, an 8-year-old Rottweiler from Brisbane. His owner noticed his normally golden-coloured wee on the lawn was suddenly dark amber, and the next day there was a small clot. Max was still wagging his tail, eating his dinner, but hed stared at his water bowl a bit longer than usual. A quick vet trip showed a simple lower UTI — most likely from E. coli bacteria. After a urine culture and a week of dog-specific antibiotics, Maxs wee was back to normal. The key? His owner didnt wait until Max was obviously sick; they had a proper diagnosis early.

Your At-Home Self-Check: What Aussie Owners Can Do Tonight

Before you ring the vet in the morning, try these four observations. Theyll help your vet pinpoint the issue faster:

  1. Slip a clean ladle or container under them: If your dog will let you, catch a mid-stream wee sample in a spotlessly clean container. Pop it in the fridge (label it — your family will thank you!) and take it to the vet within a couple of hours. Even a photo of the stream on grass can help.
  2. Check the gums and underside of the lip: Gently lift their lip. Nice and pink? Good. Pale, dry, or tacky? That can signal dehydration or worse, and its worth a call.
  3. Feel their belly with a flat palm: With your dog standing, gently run your hand along the lower tummy, just in front of the back legs. Do they flinch, tense up, or give you a warning look? That might mean bladder pain. A hard, swollen abdomen is a red flag.
  4. Count the doggy groans: Not scientific, but note if they grumble when lying down, if they pace at night, or if theyve started licking their private area more than an old boy trimming his nails. These are subtle pain signs that big dogs often hide until its uncomfortable.

Dont try to press firmly or play vet; youre just collecting clues to share with the professional.

Get urgent veterinary help now if your dog stops urinating entirely, cannot stand, has a visibly swollen or hard belly, vomits repeatedly, or seems extremely weak or wobbly. These signs can point to a blocked urinary tract or acute kidney injury, which can become life-threatening within hours.

When to Call Your Vet Right Away

Most times, a vet appointment in the next day or two is perfectly okay. But there are genuine red lines where waiting isnt safe.

If your dog:

  • Stops urinating entirely — they keep squatting but nothing comes out, or you havent seen any wee for 12-18 hours.
  • Vomiting repeatedly — not just an empty tummy heave, but ongoing and paired with lethargy.
  • Seems extremely weak or wobbly — they cant stand, or their back legs keep giving way.
  • Has a visibly swollen, hard belly — it looks bloated or they groan when its touched.

Do not wait — contact your vet or your nearest emergency vet clinic immediately. These signs can point to an acute kidney injury or a blocked urinary tract, which can become life-threatening within hours. For general kidney warning signs, see our kidney disease guide for senior dogs.

What Happens at the Vet: No Fancy Jargon, Just the Facts

When you go in, your vet will likely:

  • Run a quick urinalysis — dipping a test strip in that wee sample you so bravely collected. It checks for blood, protein, glucose, and whether the urine is too dilute or concentrated.
  • Examine your dogs belly and prostate (for boys).
  • Possibly recommend blood tests to see how kidneys and other organs are functioning, especially if your dog is over 7 years.
  • Suggest an ultrasound or X-ray if they suspect stones or a more complicated bladder wall issue.

Treatment could be as simple as antibiotics and some pain relief, a diet change to dissolve certain stones, or a management plan for kidney support. None of this is out of the ordinary for a big dogs golden years.

Keep this in mind: Dark or bloody urine in a big senior dog is a prompt to look closer, not a panic button. Most causes are treatable, and the sooner you have a urine sample and a vet review, the more options you keep open. Watch for the red lines (no urine, swollen belly, vomiting, weakness), and trust your instincts when something feels off.

Frequently Asked Questions

Could dark urine just be dehydration from a hot Aussie day?

Yes, partly. Concentrated urine can look deep yellow or amber, and it might even smell stronger. But if you offer fresh water and it doesnt lighten up within a few hours, or if theres any pinkish tint, its not just dehydration. A simple urine test (specific gravity) can tell the difference, so a vet check is the safest bet.

My large senior dog has always had slightly dark wee — should I worry?

Not all dark urine is a crisis, but the baseline can change as dogs age because kidneys become less efficient at concentrating urine. If you havent had it checked in the last 6 months, its worth a vet visit to measure kidney values and rule out a low-grade infection thats been simmering. Older large breeds deserve a wellness screen just like we do.

Ive read that certain foods or supplements can turn urine red — is that possible?

Its unlikely with typical dog diets, and red colour from beetroot or berries is extremely rare in dogs. True red or rusty-brown colour is almost always blood or blood breakdown products. Dont gamble on a food theory; let your vet confirm with a proper test.
