Heart Health

Senior Large-Breed Dog Coughing, Then Dry-Heaving: Could It Be Their Heart?

August 02, 2026 · 6 min read · PawWell Editorial

Yes, a cough that ends in a dry heave or gag can sometimes be related to heart disease—but the sound alone cannot tell you the cause.

Heart failure may lead to fluid building up in or around the lungs, which can make breathing faster or harder and may trigger coughing. An enlarged heart can also affect nearby airways in some dogs.

However, throat and lung problems can produce an almost identical cough-gag pattern. In older large dogs, possibilities include laryngeal paralysis, bronchitis, infection, irritation from food or water, and occasionally something caught in the throat.

So, don’t panic—but don’t guess either. If this is a new or recurring cough, especially when paired with faster breathing, reduced stamina or weakness, arrange a veterinary check.

An Illustrative Scenario

The following is a fictional example, not a real patient.

Banjo, a nine-year-old Labrador, began coughing at night. Each episode ended with him stretching out his neck and making a gagging sound, as though he were trying to bring something up.

At first, his family wondered whether he had picked up kennel cough. Then they noticed another small change: Banjo was falling behind on his usual walks and taking longer to settle afterwards.

At the clinic, the vet listened to his heart and lungs and recommended chest x-rays and further heart testing. The examination showed that Banjo’s symptoms needed cardiac investigation rather than home treatment for a simple throat irritation.

The important part of Banjo’s story is not that every cough means heart disease. It is that a cough, gagging and reduced stamina can overlap across several conditions—and the cause cannot safely be confirmed from the sound alone.

How Heart Disease Can Cause Coughing

Large and giant breeds can develop dilated cardiomyopathy, often shortened to DCM. With DCM, the heart muscle becomes weakened and the heart may enlarge, making it less effective at pumping blood.

If the disease progresses to congestive heart failure, pressure can build behind the heart and fluid may leak into the lungs. This is called pulmonary oedema. Affected dogs are often more noticeably breathless than coughy: they may breathe faster while resting, work harder to breathe, struggle to get comfortable or become unusually tired.

Coughing can occur with heart disease, but a dry cough, wet cough, honk or gag is not enough to diagnose heart failure. Dogs often swallow material they cough up, so the sound does not reliably tell you whether fluid is present.

Some dogs with heart disease may also have a separate airway problem. That is one reason vets commonly use chest x-rays and, when appropriate, an echocardiogram rather than judging the cough by sound alone.

Other Conditions That Can Sound Similar

A cough followed by gagging is not specific to the heart. Other possible causes include:

  • Laryngeal paralysis: Often seen in older, larger dogs, including Labradors. You may also notice noisy breathing, a changed bark, heavy panting or coughing around food and water.
  • Respiratory infection: Kennel cough and other infections can produce repeated bouts of harsh coughing followed by retching.
  • Chronic airway disease: Bronchitis and other lung conditions may cause persistent coughing, even when the heart is normal.
  • Throat irritation or a foreign object: Grass seeds, food and other material can sometimes irritate or obstruct the throat.
  • Aspiration or swallowing difficulties: Coughing that regularly appears after eating or drinking deserves veterinary attention.
  • Reverse sneezing: This usually sounds more like repeated snorting or forceful inhaling, but owners sometimes mistake it for choking or gagging.

A short video of an episode can be extremely helpful to your vet, provided filming does not delay emergency care.

Emergency Signs: When Not to Wait

Go to your vet or an emergency veterinary clinic immediately if your dog:

  • Is struggling to breathe while resting
  • Is breathing with obvious abdominal effort
  • Cannot settle or lie down comfortably because of their breathing
  • Is breathing with an open mouth when they are not hot or exercising
  • Stands with the neck extended and elbows held away from the chest
  • Has pale, grey or blue-tinged gums
  • Collapses, faints, cannot stand or seems poorly responsive
  • Coughs up pink or blood-tinged froth
  • Has a suddenly swollen abdomen together with weakness or breathing difficulty
  • Has a sudden, relentless coughing or choking episode and cannot catch their breath

If your dog is already struggling to breathe, do not spend time counting breaths or examining the mouth. Keep them as calm as possible, avoid pressure from a collar around the neck, call the emergency clinic and leave promptly.

When to Book a Veterinary Appointment

If the coughing is occasional and your dog is otherwise breathing comfortably, eating normally and behaving like themselves, it may not be an emergency. However, a new or recurring cough in a senior dog should still be checked within the next few days.

Contact your vet sooner if:

  • The episodes are becoming more frequent
  • The cough regularly happens at night or while resting
  • Your dog tires more easily on walks
  • There is weakness, fainting or reduced appetite
  • Their resting breathing rate is rising
  • They already have a heart murmur or diagnosed heart condition

Heart disease is only one possible explanation. Booking an examination early gives your vet a better chance of identifying the real cause before the symptoms become more difficult to manage.

Three Useful Checks You Can Do Today

1. Count the Sleeping Breathing Rate

Wait until your dog is fully asleep and is not panting, dreaming or moving around.

Watch the chest rise and fall. One rise and fall counts as one breath. Count for a full 60 seconds and write down the result.

Many healthy dogs—and dogs with stable, well-controlled heart disease—have a sleeping or calm resting rate between approximately 15 and 30 breaths per minute.

A rate that is consistently above 30 breaths per minute, or is clearly rising above your dog’s normal pattern, should be reported to your vet. One slightly high reading after activity, heat, stress or dreaming does not diagnose heart failure.

Breathing effort matters as much as the number. Laboured breathing at rest is an emergency even if you have not completed the count.

2. Check the Gum Colour

When your dog is calm, gently lift the lip. Healthy gums are usually moist and pink, although normal pigmentation varies between dogs.

Pale, white, grey or blue-tinged gums—particularly when accompanied by weakness or breathing difficulty—need urgent veterinary attention.

3. Record the Pattern

Write down:

  • What time the coughing happens
  • Whether your dog was asleep, resting or exercising
  • Whether it follows eating or drinking
  • How long each episode lasts
  • Whether it ends in gagging, swallowing or bringing anything up
  • Whether breathing seems faster afterwards
  • Any changes in energy, appetite, bark or walking tolerance

If it is safe, record a short video. The pattern and sound may help your vet distinguish coughing from gagging, reverse sneezing, choking or noisy breathing.

Do not give human cough medicine or change any prescribed heart medication unless your vet specifically tells you to do so.

What Might the Vet Check?

Your vet may begin by listening to the heart and lungs, checking the pulse, looking at gum colour and assessing breathing rate and effort.

Depending on what they find, they may recommend:

  • Chest x-rays to assess the heart, lungs and larger airways
  • An echocardiogram to examine the heart’s structure and pumping action
  • An ECG to look for an abnormal heart rhythm
  • Blood pressure measurement
  • Blood tests, sometimes including a cardiac biomarker such as NT-proBNP
  • Additional airway or throat investigations if the heart does not appear to be the cause

These tests serve different purposes. For example, an echocardiogram can show how the heart is working, while chest x-rays are particularly useful when the vet needs to look for changes in the lungs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can coughing followed by dry-heaving come from something other than the heart?

Yes. Respiratory infections, bronchitis, laryngeal paralysis, swallowing problems, throat irritation and foreign material can all cause coughing or gagging.

Laryngeal paralysis is particularly worth considering in an older large-breed dog if the cough is accompanied by noisy breathing, a changed bark, heavy panting or trouble around food and water.

Because these conditions can sound similar at home, a veterinary examination is the safest way to separate them.

Which large breeds are more prone to DCM?

DCM is recognised more often in breeds including Dobermanns, Boxers, Great Danes and Irish Wolfhounds, although it can occur in other breeds and mixed-breed dogs.

Labradors, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds and Rottweilers can also develop heart disease, but being a senior large-breed dog does not automatically mean a new cough is cardiac.

Your dog’s individual history, examination and test results matter more than breed alone.

How will the vet know whether the cough is heart-related?

The vet will consider the whole picture—not just the sound of the cough. Breathing rate and effort, exercise tolerance, heart rhythm, lung sounds and gum colour all provide useful clues.

Chest x-rays may show heart enlargement, lung changes or fluid. An echocardiogram can assess the heart muscle, valves and pumping function. An ECG may detect rhythm problems, and selected blood tests can provide additional information.

No single home observation can confirm heart failure.

Is more than 30 breaths per minute always an emergency?

Not necessarily. Heat, recent activity, stress, panting and dreaming can temporarily raise the rate.

Repeat the measurement when your dog is fully asleep. If it remains above 30 breaths per minute, is increasing over several readings or is accompanied by coughing or reduced stamina, contact your vet.

If breathing is visibly difficult, your dog cannot settle, or the gums are pale, grey or blue, treat it as an emergency regardless of the number.

A Final Word for Worried Dog Parents

Hearing your big old mate cough and gag can be frightening. The reassuring part is that you do not have to work out the cause by yourself.

Watch their breathing, note any changes and arrange a veterinary check. If something feels genuinely wrong—especially if your dog is struggling to breathe—trust that instinct and seek help.

You are not being fussy. You are noticing a change in a dog who cannot explain how they feel, and that is exactly what a caring owner should do.
