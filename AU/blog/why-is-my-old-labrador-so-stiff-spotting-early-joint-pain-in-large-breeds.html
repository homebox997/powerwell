Arthritis

Why Is My Old Labrador So Stiff? Spotting Early Joint Pain in Large Breeds

July 31, 2026 · 6 min read

It's a crisp winter morning in Melbourneice on the lawn, kettle on for your first cuppa. You call Banjo, your ten-year-old Labrador, to come for brekkie. Usually he'd barrel down the hallway, tail going like a propeller. But this morning he's slow. He heaves himself up with a grunt, stands for a moment as if his legs need to remember their job, then pads stiffly towards you. You put it down to the cold, to him getting older. And you're probably half rightbut there might be more to the story.

Large breeds like Labradors, German Shepherds, Rottweilers, and Golden Retrievers are built for action. But those big, loving frames also mean a lot of weight on the joints, and as the years roll on, wear and tear can sneak in. The early whispers of joint pain are often so subtle we miss themor we mistake them for a dog simply slowing down. Let's dig into what's normal and what's not, so you can give your faithful friend the most comfortable golden years.

Is It Just 'Old Age' or Is There Pain?

One of the biggest myths we hear is that a stiff, quiet older dog is just getting on. In reality, many senior dogs are living with chronic pain from osteoarthritis (OA)a condition where the cushioning cartilage in the joints gradually wears away, causing inflammation and discomfort. It's not the same as a few grey whiskers or a longer nap; it's a treatable condition. Dismissing it as normal ageing can leave your mate hurting in silence.

Think of it like this: you'd expect a few more wrinkles and a slower pace in your 70s, but you wouldn't expect to wince every time you stood up. The same goes for your dog. The key is to tease apart natural, comfortable slowing from the subtle signs of joint pain.

The Early, Easy-to-Miss Signs

Joint pain often starts as a whisper, not a shout. You won't necessarily see a dramatic limp or yelp. Instead, watch for these gentle changesthey're the ones most Aussie owners initially brush aside:

  • Stiffness after rest, especially on cold mornings. Your dog might struggle to rise from their bed, stand bunched up for a few seconds, or take unusually short, careful steps for the first minute. This is because the joint fluid thickens when the body is still, and cold weather can make it feel stiffer. If you see this regularly, it's worth noting.
  • Lagging behind on walks. He used to power ahead to the beach, tail wagging. Now he drops back, stops to sniff far more often (a clever cop-out), or sits down partway. If you're constantly shortening your route just to get him home, that's not just lazinessit could be pain.
  • Hesitation on stairs or jumping into the car. A big dog who once bounded into the back of the ute might now pause, give you a look, and wait for a boost. Or he might follow you up the steps one at a time, with an obvious pause between each. This reluctance isn't about being naughty; it's about discomfort.
  • Small behaviour changes. An easygoing Rottweiler might become a bit grumpy when you touch a sore hip. A German Shepherd might withdraw, spending more time alone rather than under your feet. A Labrador might circle excessively before lying down, trying to find a comfy position. Some dogs even lick or nibble at a painful joint, leaving a telltale wet patch of fur.
  • Subtle limping or a 'funny' gait. It might come and gomaybe after a longer walk, or first thing in the morning. You might notice a swaying hip movement or a shortened stride. This can be an early sign of osteoarthritis or even hip dysplasia, where the hip joint didn't form properly in puppyhood and now causes early wear.

A Story from the Clinic

Illustrative scenario not a real patient. Moose, a 10-year-old Rottweiler, had always been Sarah's shadow. But over a few months, Sarah noticed Moose wasn't jumping onto the couch for cuddles anymore. He'd rest more after their evening stroll, and on chilly Toowoomba mornings, he seemed to take ages to get up. Sarah thought, He's just an old bloke now. When she finally mentioned it during a routine vet check, the vet found Moose had early arthritis in his elbows and hips. With a tailored planweight management, a joint supplement containing fish oil and green-lipped mussel extract, and short, gentle walksMoose was soon hopping up for cuddles again. Sarah said, I can't believe I nearly missed it.

If your dog shows any of these signs regularly, especially for more than two weeks, it's the right moment for a gentle vet check. Catching it early lets you get ahead of the pain, not just chase it.

Practical, Do-Tonight Care for Your Senior Mate

Even before a diagnosis, you can make small changes that melt away some of the daily discomfort. These are safe, loving steps you can take starting this evening:

  • Warmth is medicine. In winter, give him a thick, supportive bed away from draughts. A heated pet mat (used safely) or a warm blanket fresh from the dryer can soothe stiff joints. If he sleeps outside, reconsiderindoor warmth makes a world of difference.
  • Non-slip floors. Hard surfaces like tiles or floorboards can be frightening and slippery for a wobbly dog. Lay down cheap yoga mats, rugs, or runner carpets in high-traffic areas. It gives them confidence to move without fear of falling.
  • Ramps and steps. For the car, a folding ramp can save him a painful jump. For the back steps, a gentle portable ramp or even a temporary step can ease the strain. Many pet supply shops now have lightweight, affordable options.
  • Keep him lean. Every extra kilo presses down on those joints. You should be able to feel his ribs easily under a thin layer of fleshif not, it's time to chat with your vet about a senior-friendly portion plan. Even a small weight loss can bring huge relief.
  • Gentle, consistent exercise. Long romps on hard sand might be out, but a leisurely lead walk on grass or a swim at a dog-friendly beach is gold. Swimming takes the weight off joints while keeping muscles strong. Aim for shorter, more frequent walks rather than one epic weekend hike.
  • Consider a joint supplement. Omega-3 fatty acids from quality fish oil, krill oil, or green-lipped mussel can help cool the inflammation that causes pain. These aren't overnight miracles, but many owners notice their dogs moving more comfortably after a few weeks. Always choose a product made for dogs and check with your vet first.
  • Massage and gentle stretching. Many dogs love a soft, slow massage around the hips, shoulders, and along the spinejust use flat palms and watch his body language. If he leans in, you're doing good. Avoid pressing directly on painful, swollen spots.

When to See the Vet

There's no shame in needing professional help. In fact, it's the kindest step you can take. A vet can confirm whether joint pain is present, rule out other issues (like ligament injuries or infections), and suggest a pain management plan that might include medication, supplements, physiotherapy, or even newer treatments like laser therapy. Never give your dog human painkillersmany are toxic to dogs.

Also, be alert to the red flags that need immediate attention. If your dog suddenly refuses to put weight on a leg, seems sick or feverish (warm ears, panting, lethargy), or has a hot, swollen joint, that could be an infection in the joint, not simple arthritis. Don't waitget to the vet straight away.

But for most big old dogs, the message is simple: stiffness and slowing down aren't just signs of getting older. They're often signs that your mate's body is aching and could do with a hand. You don't need to rush to the vet tonight over one slow morning, but if you're noticing a pattern, trust your gut. A few simple changes can turn those stiff, silent days into comfortable, tail-wagging ones. Your loyal old dog has given you years of love; now you can give him the gift of moving with ease.
