Is Your Senior Small Dog Coughing at Night? It’s Not Just Old Age – It Could Be Their Heart

心脏护理 · Published July 26, 2026 · 6 min read

---

Picture this: you’re settling in for the night, and your little old dog – maybe a Cavalier, Dachshund, or Mini Poodle – starts that soft, hacking cough again. It’s worse when they’re lying down, and you’ve noticed they pant heavily even while snoozing. It’s tempting to chalk it up to ‘just getting older’, but here’s the truth: those signs are often the first whisper of a heart that’s working too hard.

We’re going to walk through what’s really going on, how you can check your dog at home tonight, and why catching it early is the kindest thing you can do for your furry family member.

Why Nighttime Coughing in Older Small Dogs Shouldn’t Be Ignored

In breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Dachshunds, Miniature Poodles, Chihuahuas and Mini Schnauzers, a cough that gets worse at night or during rest is a classic red flag. The culprit is often a leaky heart valve – a condition called mitral valve disease. Think of it like a door that doesn’t shut properly, letting blood flow the wrong way. Over time, that leakiness forces fluid to build up on the lungs (your vet might call it ‘fluid on the lungs’ or congestive heart failure).

So why at night? When your dog lies down, that fluid spreads across the lungs more easily, triggering a cough. Heavy panting in their sleep and getting tired halfway around the block are the same story – the heart can’t keep up with even gentle exercise. If you notice these three things together – night cough, panting at rest, tiring easily – it’s time to listen carefully. Many Aussie pet parents have mistaken these signals for a dusty backyard or a warm summer night, when really the heart needed help.

The Simple At-Home Check That Could Save Your Dog’s Life

There’s one number you can track at home that veterinarians swear by: your dog’s sleeping respiratory rate (SRR). It’s just counting how many times their chest rises in one minute while they’re deeply asleep – not just dozing, but truly out.

How to do it tonight:
  • Wait until your dog is in a deep sleep, flat on their side.
  • Watch their chest rise and fall. Each rise is one breath.
  • Count for 60 seconds (or 30 seconds and multiply by two).
  • Write the number down, and aim to check it around the same time each day.
What’s normal? A relaxed, healthy dog usually breathes under 30 breaths per minute. If you’re consistently seeing numbers above 35–40, that’s a loud alarm bell. A sleeping rate over 40 can mean fluid is already building up, even if your dog seems fine otherwise. Grab your phone and jot down a few days of readings – your vet will find this gold.

Breeds We Need to Watch Extra Closely

You won’t be surprised to hear this, but Cavalier King Charles Spaniels are the poster pups for this heart valve trouble. By about 6–8 years of age, many already have a murmur, and by 10, the vast majority show some degree of the disease. Other small breeds with a strong family tendency include Dachshunds, Miniature Poodles, Chihuahuas, and Miniature Schnauzers. If you share your home with one of these sweet little souls, think of heart checks like an annual dental – just part of the routine.

Even if your dog is a delightful mixed breed with a dash of one of these, the genetics can still play a role. So don’t assume a crossbred ‘bitzer’ is exempt.

From Murmur to Treatment: Why Early Screening Gives You More Time

Veterinary cardiologists have mapped out clear stages for this condition, and knowing where your dog sits on that path makes all the difference. There’s a landmark study called the EPIC trial that changed how we treat this disease forever. It showed that starting a medication called pimobendan at Stage B2 – that’s when a vet hears a murmur and sees the heart has enlarged on an ultrasound, but the dog still seems perfectly well – delayed actual heart failure by around 15 months. That’s more time in the backyard, more walks along the beach, more cuddles on the couch.

The stages go like this:

  • Stage A: Breeds at high risk, no changes yet.
  • Stage B1: A murmur is present but the heart is still normal size.
  • Stage B2: Murmur plus an enlarged heart – the sweet spot for early treatment.
  • Stage C: Past or current symptoms of heart failure (cough, panting, fluid on lungs).
  • Stage D: Hard-to-manage heart failure needing multiple medications.

Your local vet can listen, and if they hear a murmur, they may recommend a chest X-ray or refer you for a heart scan. It’s not just about treating when they’re sick; it’s about keeping them healthy and comfortable for longer.

What Your Vet Can Do: Medications That Help

If your dog reaches Stage C, there are three main players that work as a team:

  1. Water tablets (diuretics like furosemide): These help the kidneys flush out extra fluid, clearing the lungs so breathing feels easy again. Your vet will fine-tune the dose carefully based on that sleeping respiratory rate you’re tracking at home.
  2. Pimobendan: This clever drug both strengthens each heartbeat and opens up blood vessels, so the heart doesn’t have to push so hard. It’s a cornerstone of care, and the earlier it’s started in the right cases, the better the outcome.
  3. ACE inhibitors (like enalapril): These gently lower blood pressure and ease fluid build-up. They’re used alongside the other two to take the strain off the heart.

All of these require regular vet visits to check kidney function and adjust doses. But the payoff is huge – your little mate can breathe comfortably again and get back to enjoying life.

Winston’s Story: A Real Aussie Cavalier with Heart Failure

Winston is an 11-year-old Cavalier King Charles Spaniel from Newcastle. His owner noticed he’d started hacking at night and seemed rustier on his morning walk. Then came the panting while he was resting on his bed. A quick breathing count showed his sleeping rate was up around 44 breaths per minute. The vet listened, heard a loud murmur, and an X-ray showed fluid on the lungs. Winston was in Stage C congestive heart failure.

But here’s the good news: once Winston started on water tablets and pimobendan, his breathing settled within days. His owner now tracks his sleeping rate every morning and knows exactly when to call the vet. Winston still snuggles on the couch each evening, tail thumping.

Tonight, Show Your Old Dog Some Extra Love (and Count Their Breaths)

There’s a vet’s rule of thumb that’s stuck with me: a nocturnal cough plus panting at rest in a small old dog is heart disease until proven otherwise. It’s not age. It’s not a cold. It’s a sign that with the right help, you can give your dog more comfortable months or even years.

Your action plan tonight:

  • Count that sleeping respiratory rate and jot it down.
  • If it’s above 35–40, or you’ve spotted a nighttime cough, ring your vet and ask for a heart check.
  • If your dog is an at-risk breed, talk to your vet about early screening – even if they seem fine.

We all want our dogs to grow old with waggy tails and bright eyes. Paying attention to the quiet signals at night is one of the simplest, most loving ways to do exactly that.
