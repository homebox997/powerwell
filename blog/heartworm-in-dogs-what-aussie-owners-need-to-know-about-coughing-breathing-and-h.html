Heartworm in Dogs: More Than Just a Cough

G’day, fellow dog lovers! If you’ve ever watched your dog cough after a run at the beach or seem a bit puffed on a warm day, you might wonder if it’s just dust or a bit of excitement. But sometimes, that cough can be a sign of something more serious – heartworm disease.

Heartworm is spread by mosquitoes, and unfortunately, it’s a real problem here in Australia. The good news? It’s totally preventable. But what many owners don’t realise is that heartworm can cause hidden damage, especially to your dog’s lungs and heart. Let’s break it down in plain English.

What Exactly Is Heartworm Disease?

Heartworm is a parasite that lives in your dog’s heart and the big blood vessels leading to the lungs. Over time, these worms can cause inflammation, scarring, and high blood pressure in the lungs – a condition called pulmonary hypertension (PH for short). It’s a bit like having a traffic jam in your dog’s chest. If left untreated, it can lead to heart failure.

The Sneaky Signs: From Mild to Severe

Here’s the tricky bit – many dogs with heartworm show no obvious symptoms early on. They might just have a mild cough that comes and goes. As the disease gets worse, you might notice:

  • Weight loss (even if they’re eating well)
  • Trouble breathing or panting more than normal
  • A swollen belly (fluid buildup)
  • Fainting or collapsing

In severe cases, you might see a swelling in the neck (jugular vein) or a distended tummy – these are signs of right-sided heart failure. Scary stuff, I know. But catching it early makes all the difference.

The Hidden Danger: Pulmonary Hypertension

A recent study of 50 heartworm-infected dogs found that 40% had moderate to severe pulmonary hypertension – even if they weren’t showing clear signs. That’s almost half! So relying on symptoms alone can be risky.

Vets use a special ultrasound (echocardiography) to check for PH. But they’ve also found a clever blood test that helps: measuring a substance called NT-proBNP. Think of it as a ‘stress signal’ from the heart. If levels are high, it means the heart is working too hard, and there’s likely PH.

Why This Matters for Your Dog

If your dog has heartworm and undiagnosed PH, starting treatment too quickly can be dangerous. The medication that kills adult worms can release debris that blocks blood vessels, making breathing worse and even causing collapse. That’s why vets always want to check the heart and lungs first.

Actionable advice for tonight:
  • If your dog has a cough that won’t go away, especially after exercise, book a vet visit.
  • Ask your vet about a heartworm test and an NT-proBNP blood test if they suspect PH.
  • Never start heartworm treatment without a full check-up, including an ultrasound if recommended.

Prevention Is Your Best Mate

The easiest way to avoid all this? Year-round heartworm prevention. In Australia, mosquitoes are active most of the year, especially in warmer areas like Queensland, the NT, and northern NSW. A simple chew or spot-on treatment once a month can save your dog from a world of hurt.

Quick tips for Aussie owners:
  • Set a monthly reminder on your phone for heartworm prevention.
  • Keep your dog’s heartworm prevention up to date, even in winter.
  • If you adopt a rescue dog, ask the shelter about their heartworm history.

The Take-Home Message

Heartworm disease isn’t just about coughing – it’s a whole-body problem that can damage your dog’s lungs and heart without you even knowing. With a bit of prevention and regular check-ups, you can keep your best mate happy and healthy.
