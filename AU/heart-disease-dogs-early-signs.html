Heart Disease in Dogs: Early Warning Signs Every Aussie Owner Must Know

Heart disease affects approximately 10% of dogs in Australia, with rates climbing to over 30% in senior breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels. Learning to recognise early warning signs could add years to your dog's life.

💡 Key Takeaway

Heart disease in dogs often develops silently over months or years. The earlier you detect it, the more treatment options are available. With modern medications and careful management, many dogs with heart conditions live comfortable, active lives for years after diagnosis.

Why Early Detection Matters

Unlike a broken leg or a skin infection, heart disease in dogs rarely announces itself with sudden, obvious symptoms. Instead, it creeps in quietly — a slight decrease in energy here, a bit more panting there — easily dismissed as "just getting older" or "the warm Australian weather."

But here's what many dog owners don't realise: by the time obvious symptoms appear, significant heart damage may have already occurred. The heart is remarkably good at compensating for problems, masking symptoms until it can no longer keep up. This is why regular veterinary check-ups and knowing the subtle early signs are so crucial.

The good news? Veterinary cardiology has advanced enormously in recent years. Medications like pimobendan, when started early, can significantly extend both lifespan and quality of life for dogs with certain heart conditions. Early detection doesn't just buy time — it buys good time.

⚠️ When to Seek Emergency Care Immediately

If your dog experiences sudden collapse, severe difficulty breathing, blue-tinged gums, or extreme lethargy (unable to stand), seek emergency veterinary care immediately. These could signal acute heart failure and require urgent treatment.

Early Warning Signs You Shouldn't Ignore

The tricky thing about canine heart disease is that early symptoms are often subtle and easy to miss. Here's what to watch for:

Changes in Breathing

  • Increased panting — especially when it's not hot or after minimal exercise
  • Shortness of breath — laboured breathing or panting that seems excessive for the activity level
  • Coughing after exercise or excitement — this can indicate fluid building up in or around the lungs
  • Breathing faster at rest — normal resting respiratory rate is under 30 breaths per minute; consistently higher rates warrant investigation

Changes in Energy and Behaviour

  • Tiring more quickly on walks — stopping to rest, lagging behind, or cutting walks short
  • Reluctance to play or exercise — a once-enthusiastic ball chaser showing disinterest
  • Slowing down on stairs — taking stairs one at a time, pausing on landings
  • Restlessness at night — pacing, unable to settle, seeking cool surfaces
  • Fainting or collapsing — brief episodes of weakness or collapse, especially after excitement

Physical Signs

  • Distended abdomen — a pot-bellied appearance can indicate fluid accumulation from heart failure
  • Weight loss despite normal appetite — the body working harder to compensate
  • Gum colour changes — pale, grey, or blue-tinged gums suggest poor circulation
  • Weak or irregular pulse — something your vet will check during examinations
🐕❤️
Monitoring your dog's resting breathing rate at home is one of the most valuable tools for early detection. Count breaths for 15 seconds and multiply by 4 — rates consistently above 30 per minute warrant a vet visit.

Understanding Disease Progression

Heart disease typically progresses through stages. Recognising which stage your dog is in helps you and your vet make better treatment decisions:

🟢 Stage A (At Risk)
No symptoms. High-risk breed or family history. Regular monitoring recommended.
🟡 Stage B (Murmur Detected)
Heart murmur present but no symptoms. No heart enlargement. Monitoring every 6–12 months.
🔴 Stage C (Heart Failure)
Clinical signs present (coughing, difficulty breathing, exercise intolerance). Medication essential.
⚫ Stage D (Refractory)
Severe heart failure despite treatment. Intensive care required. Palliative options considered.

Diagnostic Tests Australian Vets Use

When heart disease is suspected, your veterinarian may recommend several tests to understand the extent of the problem:

🩺 Auscultation (Listening)
Using a stethoscope to detect heart murmurs, abnormal rhythms, or fluid in the lungs. Often the first indication of heart disease.
Included in consultation
📸 Chest X-rays
Shows heart size, shape, and fluid in the lungs. Essential for assessing heart enlargement.
$150–300
💓 Echocardiogram (Ultrasound)
The gold standard for diagnosing heart disease. Shows heart chamber sizes, valve function, and pumping ability in real-time.
$300–600
📈 ECG (Electrocardiogram)
Records the heart's electrical activity. Detects irregular heartbeats and certain rhythm disorders.
$50–150

Australian pet owners should note that many veterinary practices now offer referral to specialist veterinary cardiologists for complex cases. Major cities including Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane, Perth, and Adelaide have dedicated cardiology services with advanced imaging capabilities.

High-Risk Breeds in Australia

While any dog can develop heart disease, certain breeds are predisposed. If you have one of these breeds, regular heart checks should be part of your preventive care routine:

Cavalier King Charles Spaniel

The most heart-vulnerable breed in Australia. Over 50% develop mitral valve disease by age 10. Annual heart checks are essential from age 4. Early intervention with medications like pimobendan has been shown to extend life by approximately 15 months when started at the right stage.

Doberman Pinscher

Prone to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), a condition where the heart becomes enlarged and weak. Screening with echocardiograms is recommended from age 2, as sudden death can occur without warning in advanced cases.

Small Breeds Generally

Chihuahuas, Poodles, Dachshunds, and other small breeds have higher rates of mitral valve disease. The risk increases significantly after age 10. Annual murmur screening is recommended for all seniors.

Large and Giant Breeds

Irish Wolfhounds, Great Danes, and Boxers have elevated risk for dilated cardiomyopathy. Newfoundland dogs are prone to subaortic stenosis. Regular cardiac screening is vital for these breeds.

Prevention and Heart-Healthy Habits

While you can't change your dog's genetics, there's plenty you can do to support heart health:

Maintain Ideal Weight

Obesity puts tremendous strain on the heart. Work with your vet to determine your dog's ideal body condition score and adjust food portions accordingly. Even a 10% reduction in excess weight can significantly reduce cardiac workload.

Regular, Appropriate Exercise

Consistent moderate exercise strengthens the heart muscle. However, if your dog has been diagnosed with heart disease, discuss appropriate exercise levels with your vet — too much can be harmful. Shorter, more frequent walks are often better than long hikes.

Dental Health

There's a documented link between dental disease and heart disease in dogs. Bacteria from infected gums can enter the bloodstream and settle on heart valves, causing endocarditis. Regular dental cleanings under anaesthesia are an investment in heart health.

Annual Veterinary Examinations

For senior dogs (over 7 years in large breeds, over 10 in small breeds), twice-yearly check-ups are ideal. Your vet will listen for murmurs and assess overall cardiovascular health. Early detection is your dog's best friend.

Know Your Dog's Normal

Learn to recognise your dog's normal breathing rate at rest, normal energy levels, and usual behaviour. You're the expert on your dog — if something seems "off," trust that instinct and seek veterinary advice. No one knows your dog better than you do.
