Senior Dog Heart Health

Senior Dog Heart Ageing: Normal Change or Heart Disease?

Written by the PawWell Editorial Team · Published 9 August 2026

Quick answer: A large dog may choose a gentler pace with age, but age alone does not explain persistent coughing, faster breathing during sleep, fainting, unusual weakness or a clear loss of stamina. There is no reliable home test that separates “normal ageing” from heart disease. The safest approach is to notice the trend, record the whole picture and let a veterinarian combine the examination with any tests that are appropriate for your dog.

The change may be so gradual that nobody can name the day it began. Your Labrador still brings the lead, but no longer asks for the longer route. Your German Shepherd pauses at the gate, then seems perfectly content to head home. On warm days, the difference is easy to explain away. On cool days, it becomes harder.

Senior dogs do change. They may lose muscle, sleep more deeply, recover more slowly from busy days and become less enthusiastic about high-energy games. The difficulty is that painful joints, heart disease, lung disease and several other illnesses can produce the same quiet editing of daily life. “He is old” describes your dog’s life stage; it does not identify the cause of a new symptom.

Do not wait for a routine appointment: Contact an emergency veterinarian if your dog is struggling to breathe at rest, cannot lie down comfortably, has blue or grey gums, collapses or faints, or deteriorates quickly. Keep movement and stress to a minimum while arranging care.

Healthy ageing is not the same as symptom-free ageing

Ageing affects the whole body. A senior dog may have less physical reserve, meaning a long outing takes more out of them than it once did. Muscle loss can make hills harder. Arthritis can shorten the stride. Hot weather can reduce anyone’s enthusiasm. These influences are real, but they should not become a blanket explanation for every change.

Heart disease is also not one single condition. Older small dogs commonly receive attention for degenerative valve disease, while several large and giant breeds are more closely watched for dilated cardiomyopathy, or DCM. DCM affects the heart muscle and can reduce its pumping ability. Other large dogs may develop rhythm problems, valve disease, disease around the heart or conditions unrelated to the heart that imitate cardiac signs.

Some disease is preclinical: changes are present, but the dog has not developed obvious symptoms. That is why “he looks fine” and “the heart is definitely fine” are not identical statements. It is also why screening decisions may differ between a healthy-looking Dobermann with recognised breed risk and a mixed-breed dog with no examination findings.

The key is not whether a dog behaves like a two-year-old. It is whether the current pattern is comfortable, predictable and stable for that individual. A new trend carries more information than comparison with another dog of the same age.

Five clues that should not be filed under “just getting old”

1. A real change in familiar exercise

Exercise intolerance means a dog can no longer comfortably manage activity that was previously routine. You may see more stops, a slower return to normal breathing, reluctance to continue, weakness or a need to lie down. It can occur with heart disease, but arthritis, airway disease, anaemia, excess body weight and poor fitness are among the other possibilities.

2. Breathing that changes during genuine rest

Breathing after a walk tells you less than breathing during quiet sleep. A repeated rise above your dog’s usual sleeping rate, or any new breathing effort, deserves veterinary advice. Effort includes strong abdominal movement, flared nostrils, a stretched neck or inability to settle. A number alone does not diagnose heart failure.

3. Collapse, fainting or sudden weakness

Fainting can occur when the brain briefly receives insufficient blood flow, including with some abnormal heart rhythms. Seizures, low blood sugar, internal bleeding and neurological problems can sometimes look similar to an owner. A video may help only if someone else is already arranging care and recording does not delay assistance.

4. A cough that becomes a pattern

Dogs cough for many reasons, and heart disease is not automatically the most likely. Airway inflammation, infection, tracheal or laryngeal problems and lung disease can all be involved. Record when the cough happens, what it sounds like, how long it lasts and whether breathing or stamina has changed. Let the examination guide the next step.

5. Abdominal swelling or unexplained weight change

Fluid can collect in the abdomen with some forms of heart failure, but abdominal enlargement can also have many non-cardiac causes. Weight loss may reflect reduced appetite, muscle loss or broader illness. Either change deserves assessment, particularly when paired with weakness, breathing changes or reduced comfort.

Why listening to the heart is useful—but not the whole answer

During a physical examination, a veterinarian listens for a murmur, abnormal rhythm and changes in heart or lung sounds. They also assess pulse quality, gum colour, breathing, body and muscle condition, abdominal changes and other clues. This broad examination matters because a dog can have more than one condition at the same time.

A heart murmur is an extra sound created by turbulent blood flow. It is a finding, not a complete diagnosis. A loud murmur does not automatically mean a dog is in heart failure, and the absence of an obvious murmur does not exclude every form of heart disease. Some rhythm and heart-muscle conditions need different tools to identify them.

An irregular rhythm heard in the clinic may lead to an electrocardiogram, which records the heart’s electrical activity. A short recording can answer some questions; intermittent rhythm changes may require longer monitoring. Breed, symptoms and examination findings help the vet decide what is worthwhile.

What different heart tests can—and cannot—show

Chest X-rays can show the outline of the heart and provide valuable information about the lungs and airways. They may help identify fluid in the lungs or another chest problem. They do not show every detail of heart structure or pumping function.

Echocardiography is an ultrasound examination of the heart. It allows a trained clinician to look at chamber size, wall movement, valves and blood flow. The ACVIM cardiomyopathy consensus describes echocardiography as the most accurate clinical test for diagnosing cardiomyopathy, but it still has to be interpreted in the context of the individual dog.

Electrocardiography and Holter monitoring examine rhythm. A Holter is a longer ambulatory recording, often used when intermittent rhythm abnormalities are suspected or when breed-specific DCM screening is discussed. A normal short tracing does not necessarily capture an event that happens only occasionally.

Blood pressure and laboratory testing help examine the wider health picture. Blood tests may be used to look for anaemia, kidney changes, electrolyte problems, inflammation or other causes of weakness and reduced stamina. Cardiac biomarkers can add information in selected cases, but they do not replace the examination and appropriate imaging.

The useful question is not “Which test is best?” It is “Which test answers the question raised by my dog’s breed, history, examination and current symptoms?” A vet may reasonably recommend different starting points for two dogs that appear similar at home.

A seven-day observation plan before a non-urgent appointment

If your dog is comfortable and has no emergency sign, a short record can make a vague change easier to assess. Do not postpone an appointment simply to complete the week.

  1. Use one familiar activity. Note how far your dog comfortably walks, how often they pause and how long they take to settle afterwards. Do not push for a personal best.
  2. Count during sleep. Record the sleeping breathing rate on several calm nights and note any visible effort.
  3. Track coughs precisely. Write down time, trigger, duration and recovery. Safely take a short video if possible.
  4. Notice appetite and body shape. Record meals left behind, weight changes and any new abdominal enlargement.
  5. List every medication and supplement. Include the label and the normal schedule. Do not add, stop or change anything for the purpose of the test.
  6. Write one sentence about comfort. “She wanted to go but stopped three times” is more useful than “old and slow.”

Use cool, familiar conditions where possible. Melbourne heat can change panting and willingness to walk, while painful joints can be worse after a long rest. Recording the context helps the vet see whether the pattern follows weather, surface, exertion or no obvious trigger.

Common shortcuts that can delay the right answer

  • “The cough is dry, so it must be the heart.” Cough sound is not a reliable home diagnosis.
  • “There is no murmur, so the heart is clear.” Not every cardiac condition produces an obvious murmur.
  • “The murmur is loud, so failure is advanced.” Murmur intensity alone does not stage every disease.
  • “A big dog naturally breathes harder.” Size does not make visible breathing distress normal.
  • “He recovered, so the faint was harmless.” Collapse or fainting requires prompt veterinary assessment even if the dog appears normal afterwards.
  • “I will try an extra heart tablet and see.” Heart medicines can affect blood pressure, hydration, electrolytes and kidney function. Follow only the treating veterinarian’s plan.

How often should a senior dog have a heart check?

There is no single schedule for every senior dog. Age, breed, past findings, medications and current symptoms all affect timing. Senior wellness examinations allow a veterinarian to compare heart and lung sounds, pulse, breathing, weight and muscle condition over time. Dogs with a recognised breed risk or an existing murmur may need a more specific plan.

Ask the veterinarian what should trigger an earlier visit. Useful questions include:

  • What change in sleeping breathing rate should prompt a call for this dog?
  • Did you hear a murmur or rhythm change, and what does it mean in this case?
  • Could pain, lungs, anaemia or another problem explain the reduced stamina?
  • Would imaging or rhythm monitoring change what we do now?
  • Which changes mean same-day care, and which mean emergency care?

Why a baseline from a well day is so valuable

Owners usually begin watching closely after something changes. A better time to learn normal is while your dog feels well. A few ordinary sleeping breathing counts, a recent weight, a note about the usual walk and a routine examination give future observations something real to compare with. The aim is not to medicalise every peaceful evening. It is to make a future change easier to recognise.

A baseline is broader than a heart rate. Most owners cannot measure a resting pulse accurately enough for it to be useful, and repeated handling may make the dog anxious. Focus on information that is easy and safe to collect: sleeping breathing, willingness to start a familiar activity, recovery afterwards, appetite, cough frequency and the ability to settle comfortably.

Season matters. A dog’s summer walk in Melbourne should not be compared casually with a cool winter morning. Record the route, temperature and pace when they influence performance. Pain matters too: a dog with sore hips may stop early without being breathless. The difference between “stopped because the back legs were stiff” and “stopped, looked weak and took much longer to recover” helps the veterinary team choose where to begin.

If a check finds no cardiac disease, the record has not been wasted. It may direct attention toward joints, lungs, blood, hormones or another cause. Good observation is not about proving your first theory; it is about describing the dog accurately enough that the right theory can emerge.

Keep this in mind: Normal ageing is usually a gradual shift that leaves a dog comfortable and engaged. Disease often reveals itself as a new, repeated or worsening loss of normal function. You do not need to decide which one it is at home. Your best contribution is a clear record of what changed, while the veterinary team connects that history with the examination and appropriate tests.

Frequently asked questions

Is a senior dog slowing down always a heart problem?

No. Joint pain, reduced fitness, excess weight, anaemia, lung disease and other problems can also reduce stamina. A consistent change from your dog’s normal routine should be assessed rather than labelled as age or heart disease at home.

Can a dog have heart disease without obvious symptoms?

Yes. Some conditions have a preclinical stage in which a dog looks well. Examination findings, breed risk and appropriate testing may identify disease before an owner sees obvious signs.

Does a heart murmur prove heart failure?

No. A murmur is a sound caused by turbulent blood flow. It can be associated with several conditions, and loudness alone does not prove heart failure or show how a dog feels.

Which heart signs need emergency care?

Seek emergency care for difficult breathing at rest, blue or grey gums, collapse or fainting, inability to lie down comfortably, or rapid deterioration.

What should I take to a senior dog heart appointment?

Bring a medication and supplement list, a short timeline, sleeping breathing-rate records if available, and videos of coughing, breathing or fainting episodes that were safe to record.
