Kidney Biopsy in Dogs: What It Reveals & Why It Matters

July 27, 2026 · 6 min read

If your senior dog has been diagnosed with kidney disease, you may have heard your veterinarian mention a kidney biopsy. This procedure can feel daunting, but for certain cases, it provides information that blood and urine tests simply cannot. Understanding what a kidney biopsy is, when it is recommended, and what it reveals can help you make informed decisions about your dog's care. In this article, we explain the procedure, its benefits, risks, and how results can change your dog's treatment plan.

What Is a Kidney Biopsy and How Is It Performed?

A kidney biopsy involves taking a tiny sample of kidney tissue for examination under a microscope. It is usually performed under general anaesthesia or deep sedation. Your veterinarian will use ultrasound guidance to locate the kidney and then insert a thin needle through the skin to collect a small piece of tissue. The sample is then sent to a veterinary pathologist for analysis. The entire procedure typically takes less than 30 minutes, and most dogs go home the same day.

When Is a Kidney Biopsy Recommended?

Not every dog with kidney disease needs a biopsy. Your veterinarian may recommend it in specific situations, such as:

  • Persistent protein-losing nephropathy: If your dog has significant protein in the urine despite dietary management and medication, a biopsy can identify the underlying cause.
  • Unexplained kidney disease in younger dogs: For dogs under 10 years of age with no clear cause of kidney disease, a biopsy can help diagnose conditions like familial nephropathy or immune-mediated disease.
  • Suspected glomerular disease: The glomeruli are the filtering units of the kidney. When they are damaged, protein leaks into the urine. A biopsy can confirm the type of glomerular disease.
  • Sudden worsening of kidney function: If your dog's kidney values decline rapidly without an obvious trigger, a biopsy may reveal an underlying inflammatory or immune-mediated process.

Always discuss the risks and benefits with your veterinarian, as a biopsy is not without risks.

What Does a Kidney Biopsy Tell Vets That Blood and Urine Tests Cannot?

Blood and urine tests are excellent for detecting kidney disease and monitoring its progression, but they have limitations. For example, creatinine is a classic marker for chronic kidney disease (CKD), but it can be affected by muscle mass, high-protein diets, and vitamin C supplements. A muscular dog may have a falsely elevated creatinine, while a dog with low muscle mass may have a falsely normal result. Similarly, SDMA (symmetric dimethylarginine) is more sensitive than creatinine but can still be influenced by certain disease states.

A kidney biopsy provides a direct look at the kidney tissue. It can reveal:

  • The type of glomerular disease: There are several types of glomerulonephritis, and each may respond differently to treatment. For example, membranous nephropathy may require immunosuppressive therapy, while minimal change disease may respond to corticosteroids.
  • Whether the disease is immune-mediated: If the biopsy shows immune complex deposition or inflammation, it suggests an immune-mediated process that may benefit from immunosuppressive drugs.
  • The extent of fibrosis and scarring: This helps determine how much kidney function has been permanently lost and guides prognosis.
  • Presence of other conditions: A biopsy can also detect amyloidosis, lymphoma, or other infiltrative diseases that blood tests might miss.

In short, a biopsy provides a definitive diagnosis that can guide treatment decisions more precisely than blood and urine tests alone.

How Do Biopsy Results Change Treatment Plans?

The results of a kidney biopsy can dramatically alter your dog's treatment plan. For instance:

  • Immunosuppressive therapy: If the biopsy confirms immune-mediated glomerulonephritis, your veterinarian may prescribe corticosteroids or other immunosuppressive drugs to reduce inflammation and protein loss.
  • Targeted medication: Certain types of glomerular disease respond better to specific medications, such as ACE inhibitors (e.g., benazepril) for reducing proteinuria or omega-3 fatty acids for their anti-inflammatory effects.
  • Avoiding unnecessary treatments: If the biopsy shows primarily scarring (fibrosis) without active inflammation, immunosuppressive drugs are unlikely to help and may cause side effects. In such cases, the focus shifts to supportive care, including a renal diet, phosphate binders, and blood pressure management.
  • Monitoring and prognosis: Knowing the exact type of kidney disease helps your veterinarian set realistic expectations. Some conditions can be managed for years, while others may progress more quickly.

For example, a 12-year-old Labrador named Dou Dou presented with frequent nighttime urination and was diagnosed with IRIS Stage 2 CKD. With a renal diet, phosphate binders, and benazepril, his creatinine dropped from 2.8 mg/dL to 2.3 mg/dL over three months, and his night-time urination improved significantly. A biopsy was not needed in his case because his disease responded well to standard therapy. However, for dogs that do not respond or have atypical features, a biopsy can be invaluable.

Risks and Aftercare

Like any procedure, a kidney biopsy carries risks. These include bleeding, infection, damage to the kidney, and the rare possibility of needing a blood transfusion. However, with ultrasound guidance and careful patient selection, the risk of serious complications is low. Most dogs recover quickly and can go home the same day. Your veterinarian will advise you to restrict your dog's activity for 24 to 48 hours and monitor for signs of discomfort, bleeding, or changes in urination. Always follow the aftercare instructions provided by your vet.

Questions Owners Should Ask Their Vet

Before deciding on a kidney biopsy, ask your veterinarian these questions:

  • Why is a biopsy recommended for my dog specifically?
  • What are the risks and how are they minimised?
  • How will the results change my dog's treatment plan?
  • Is there a chance the biopsy will not provide a clear diagnosis?
  • What is the cost of the procedure, including anaesthesia, pathology, and aftercare?
  • How should I prepare my dog for the biopsy?
  • What signs of complications should I watch for after the procedure?

Your veterinarian will be happy to discuss these points and help you make an informed decision.

A Final Word

Your senior dog's health is a journey, and sometimes a kidney biopsy can be a valuable tool to guide that journey. While it may seem like a big step, the information it provides can lead to more effective, targeted treatment and a better quality of life for your beloved companion. Always work closely with your veterinarian, ask questions, and trust your instincts. You are your dog's best advocate.
