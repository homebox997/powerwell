Your Dog's Heart Murmur: What Every Aussie Owner Needs to Know About Canine Heart Disease

Published July 26, 2026 · 5 min read

Quiet beginnings: what a heart murmur really means

Picture this: you’re at the vet for your dog’s yearly check-up and the vet says, “I can hear a heart murmur.” Your stomach drops. But don’t panic – murmurs are surprisingly common, especially in smaller breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels. A murmur is just an extra whooshing sound the blood makes as it moves through the heart. Often, it’s caused by a leaky valve, the same way a door that doesn’t quite shut lets a draught through. The good news? Catching it early means you and your vet can keep a close eye on things before any real trouble starts.

The simple home test every dog owner should know

One of the best early warning signs doesn’t need a stethoscope – you just need your dog to be sound asleep. It’s called the sleeping respiratory rate. While your dog is fully snoozing (not dreaming or twitching), count how many times their chest rises in one minute. A normal rate is under 30 breaths per minute. If you notice it creeping up and staying above 30 over a few nights, that could mean fluid is starting to build up in the lungs – a red flag for congestive heart failure. Grab your phone timer, jot it down in a notebook, and if the numbers climb, ring your vet that day. It’s a tiny habit that genuinely saves lives.

When things get serious: symptoms you can’t ignore

As heart trouble progresses, you might notice more obvious signs:

  • Coughing, especially at night or when your dog is resting.
  • Slowing down on walks – that lap around the block that used to be a sprint now leaves them puffing.
  • Breathing harder or faster, even when they haven’t been running.
  • Pale or bluish gums (check after a gentle belly rub).
  • Fainting or collapsing – this is an emergency.
  • A pot-bellied look that isn’t just extra treats; fluid can collect in the belly too.

Any of these mean the heart is struggling to keep up. Don’t take a “wait and see” approach – get them to the vet quickly.

How your vet helps: medications that make a huge difference

Once heart disease is diagnosed, your vet may start your dog on medicine to help the heart work smarter, not harder. Two of the most common are:

  • Pimobendan: This helps the heart pump more strongly and opens up blood vessels, so the heart doesn’t have to push against so much pressure. Think of it like giving the heart a bit of a helping hand, every day.
  • Furosemide (a diuretic): When fluid builds up in the lungs or belly, this medicine helps the kidneys flush it out. Your dog will pee more, which is exactly the point – it clears their breathing and makes them feel miles better.

These aren’t cures, but they can add happy, comfortable years to your dog’s life. Because they affect the kidneys, your vet will recommend regular blood tests to keep everything balanced.

The silent threat Down Under: heartworm prevention

Here in Australia, mozzies are more than a backyard nuisance – they can carry heartworm larvae straight to your dog. Once adult worms set up camp in the heart and lungs, the damage is severe and treatment is rough. Preventing it is dead easy though: a monthly chew, spot-on, or tablet like Simparica Trio, Advocate, Interceptor, or Milbemax does the job. Year-round protection is the go, even in winter. A few missed doses can leave your dog open to a completely avoidable heart disaster.

What about grain-free diets and heart disease?

You might have heard about grain-free foods being linked to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) in dogs. The truth is, the picture is still fuzzy. Researchers have raised eyebrows at diets heavy in peas, lentils, or legumes as main ingredients, but there’s no hard rule that all grain-free is bad. The smart move? Chat with your vet about what you’re feeding, especially if your dog is a large or giant breed already prone to DCM. A balanced, trusted diet is always the winner.

Does your breed need extra attention?

Some dogs are simply more likely to face heart issues:

  • Small breeds – Cavalier King Charles Spaniels, Chihuahuas, Miniature Poodles, Dachshunds, Pomeranians, and Shih Tzus often deal with leaky valves (mitral valve disease).
  • Large and giant breeds – Dobermans, Boxers, Great Danes, Irish Wolfhounds, and Cocker Spaniels are more prone to DCM, where the heart muscle becomes weak and floppy.

If your dog is on this list, talk to your vet about early screening. An ultrasound of the heart (echocardiogram) is the gold standard – it shows exactly what’s going on inside, far more clearly than just listening or taking an X-ray.

Salt: the hidden enemy for heart patients

Sodium makes the body hold onto fluid, which is the last thing a dog with heart failure needs. Steer clear of:

  • Salty deli meats, sausages, and cheese (even a little snag from the Bunnings barbie can be too much)
  • Table scraps that are seasoned
  • High-salt treats

Ask your vet about a low-salt diet. Many senior or prescription foods already have reduced sodium, making it simple to do the right thing without any guesswork.

Wrapping it up: small actions, big difference

Heart disease in dogs is more manageable than ever, but it works best when you’re part of the team. Listen at routine check-ups, count those sleeping breaths, keep up with preventatives, and don’t ignore the small signs. A little awareness goes a long way in keeping your mate’s tail wagging for years to come.
