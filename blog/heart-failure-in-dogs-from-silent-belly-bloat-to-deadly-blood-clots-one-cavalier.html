Heart Failure in Dogs: From Silent Belly Bloat to Deadly Blood Clots – One Cavalier’s Story

心脏护理 · Published July 26, 2026 · 4 min read

---

G’day, dog lovers. Picture this: you’re at the local off-leash park, the sun’s out, and your mate’s Cavalier, Charlie, is trotting around with his usual happy grin. He’s 11 now, a bit grey around the muzzle, but still full of beans – until one day he’s suddenly panting like he’s run a marathon and his tummy looks unusually round. That was the first sign of a heart problem that nearly stole him away.

Charlie’s story isn’t unique. It’s a real reminder that our dogs can hide heart trouble until it’s screaming at us. The good news? We can learn to read those whispers before they become a full-blown emergency.

The Silent Warning Signs of Heart Failure

When a dog’s heart starts to struggle, the signs can creep in so slowly you might miss them. The two biggest red flags are breathing changes and a swollen belly.

  • Breathing distress: Your dog might breathe shallow and fast even when they’re resting, or you might catch them open-mouth panting on a cool night. That’s a huge clue that fluid is building up in their lungs (something vets call pulmonary oedema).
  • Pot-belly that isn’t just extra treats: A bloated-looking tummy can mean fluid is collecting in the abdomen (ascites), putting pressure on the diaphragm and making it even harder to breathe.

In Charlie’s case, his owner noticed he was huffing and puffing for no reason, and his belly looked bigger than usual. By the time they rushed to the vet, advanced valve disease had caused both lung fluid and a fluid-filled belly. Don’t ever wait if you see these signs – a delayed trip can be fatal.

The Surprising Risk of Blood Clots in Dogs

Many paw-rents think blood clots only happen to cats. The truth is, while it’s very rare, dogs with severe heart disease can form a life-threatening clot inside the heart, too.

Charlie had a condition called mitral valve disease – a “leaky” heart valve that’s common in older small breeds. Over time his left atrium stretched enormously and the blood flow became chaotic. A tiny hole between the heart chambers (atrial septal defect) made it even wilder, damaging the heart lining. This perfect storm caused a blood clot to grow right inside his heart – something vets spotted as an irregular, bright mass on an ultrasound.

> Myth busted: Dogs absolutely can develop heart clots when the valve disease is bad enough, especially when the left atrium is massively stretched and the blood flow is turbulent. If your dog has a serious heart murmur and suddenly shows signs like struggling to breathe or limping/paralysis in the back legs, get to the vet immediately – it could be a clot on the move.

Despite medication to manage his heart failure and thin the blood, Charlie passed away 138 days later. It was heartbreaking, but his story teaches us that early detection can still buy precious, quality time.

What You Can Do Tonight to Protect Your Dog’s Heart

You don’t need a medical degree to make a difference. Start with these three simple, Aussie-friendly steps.

1. Master the Resting Breath Check

When your dog is snoozing or lying calmly, count how many breaths they take in one minute. A normal resting rate is under 30 breaths per minute. If it’s consistently higher, or if they seem to be working harder to breathe, call your vet that same day. Early action can stop lungs from filling with fluid.

2. Ditch the Salty Treats

Dogs with wobbly hearts struggle to get rid of extra salt and fluid. Feeding them high-sodium snacks can make fluid build-up worse – that means more belly bloat and laboured breathing. So, no more sausage from the barbie, ham off your sandwich, cheese cubes, or leftovers from Sunday roast. Stick to vet-recommended low-sodium heart diets and check the salt content on your dog’s treats. Your pup’s heart will thank you.

3. Book a Heart Check for At-Risk Breeds

Breeds like Cavalier King Charles Spaniels are especially prone to mitral valve disease, but any older small dog can be affected. If your furry friend is 6 or older, chat with your vet about a yearly heart ultrasound. This scan can spot an enlarged atrium, unusual clots, or that leaky valve long before your dog shows any symptoms. If something’s brewing, your vet can start early treatment and talk about whether blood-thinning medication might be needed.

Charlie’s journey was a tough one, but it doesn’t have to be your dog’s path. By staying alert for slow breathing and a swollen tummy, keeping salt off the menu, and catching heart changes early, you can help your best mate live a longer, happier, tail-wagging life. So tonight, give them a cuddle, check those breaths, and if anything feels off, give your vet a bell. They’ll be glad you did.
